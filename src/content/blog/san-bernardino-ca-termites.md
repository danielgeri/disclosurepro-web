## The Hidden Threat: Subterranean and Drywood Termites in the San Bernardino Valley

Unlike regions that deal exclusively with ground-dwelling pests, San Bernardino homebuyers face two distinct termite species that attack residential properties from opposite directions: below the foundation and above through attic vents.

### Why San Bernardino's Inland Climate and Housing Stock Accelerate Damage

Several regional, climatic, and architectural dynamics make residential properties in San Bernardino particularly prone to rapid insect damage:

- **Dual Infestation Pressure (Subterranean & Drywood):**
  - **Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** These colonies reside deep within the alluvial valley soils, tunneling upward through foundation cracks, plumbing penetrations, and perimeter stucco screeds in search of structural lumber.
  - **Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*):** These flying pests do not require soil contact. During warm late-summer and autumn afternoons—especially during gusty Santa Ana wind conditions—swarming alates fly into attic louvers, exposed rafter tails, fascia boards, and window framing.
- **Mid-Century Post-War Tract Construction:** Much of San Bernardino's housing stock in neighborhoods like Del Rosa and Kendall consists of 1940s to 1970s single-story wood-frame and stucco ranches built on concrete slab-on-grade foundations. As decades of seismic settling cause expansion joints and hairline slab cracks, subterranean termites gain direct, hidden access into interior wall sill plates.
- **Historic Wood-Frame Bungalows with Raised Foundations:** Pre-1940 homes near Downtown and Perris Hill often feature raised mudsill foundations with crawlspaces. Inadequate crawlspace ventilation, combined with leaking irrigation or poorly graded yard drainage, creates localized high-moisture pockets where subterranean colonies thrive.
- **Intense Thermal Cycling and Solar Exposure:** Extreme Inland Empire summer temperatures exceeding 100°F bake exterior roof decking and rafter tails, drying out architectural lumber. Drywood termites thrive in low-moisture framing timbers, excavating massive chambers inside attic joists without needing any water source.

### Biology and Mechanics: How Termites Compromise San Bernardino Properties

Western subterranean termites construct protective earthen shelter tubes across concrete stem walls, foundation footings, and electrical conduits. Their digestive systems utilize symbiotic microbes to extract nutrients from wood cellulose, hollowing out structural baseplates, wall studs, and subfloors along the wood grain.

In contrast, Western drywood termites bore directly into structural timbers, sealing their entry holes with a mastic made of chewed wood and liquid cement. They eat across the wood grain, creating large, clean nesting galleries. Drywood termites push distinctive six-sided, sand-grain-like fecal pellets (frass) out of tiny kick-out holes. Because drywood colonies live entirely inside the wood they consume, homeowners rarely see surface evidence until structural roof rafters, headers, or decorative exterior beams lose load-bearing capacity.

## California Real Estate Disclosure Laws: What San Bernardino Buyers Must Know

California provides robust statutory protections for residential purchasers, but buyers must understand how seller questionnaires and licensed pest inspection reports interact.

### The California Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and SPQ

Under **California Civil Code § 1102**, residential sellers must deliver a statutory **Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)** and the California Association of Realtors **Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)**.

On these legal disclosure forms, sellers are legally obligated to disclose:
- **Section II, Item C of the TDS:** Any known environmental hazards, settling, structural alterations, or wood-destroying pests/organisms.
- **Section V of the SPQ:** Specific questions regarding whether the seller has knowledge of any past or active infestations of termites, dry rot, or other pests, as well as whether any pest inspections, reports, or treatments have been completed in the preceding five years.

Under California case law (*Shapiro v. Sutherland*), sellers have an affirmative common law and statutory duty to disclose any known material facts that affect the value or desirability of the property. Failing to disclose known termite infestations or active treatments can expose sellers to damages and rescission claims.

### California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) Branch 3 Reports

In California real estate transactions, pest inspections are conducted by licensed Branch 3 Wood Destroying Pest and Organisms inspectors under the oversight of the California Department of Consumer Affairs Structural Pest Control Board.

A standard California WDO report is categorized into two crucial sections:
- **Section 1 Items:** Active infestations or visible wood decay/damage requiring immediate structural repair and chemical or fumigation eradication (e.g., live drywood termites in attic rafters, subterranean mud tubes on stem walls).
- **Section 2 Items:** Conducive conditions that are deemed likely to lead to active infestations if left uncorrected (e.g., earth-to-wood contact at patio posts, plumbing leaks near crawlspaces, lack of subfloor ventilation).

In standard California Residential Purchase Agreements (RPA), buyers and sellers negotiate whether the seller will clear Section 1 items prior to closing, or provide a financial repair credit.

### What San Bernardino Sellers Reveal vs. Conceal

Despite clear disclosure requirements, sellers often use minimized language in their SPQ narratives:
- *"Attic spot-treated by local exterminator two years ago."* (Concealing that whole-house structural fumigation tenting was recommended but rejected due to cost).
- *"Previous pest inspection completed; report on file."* (Failing to highlight that the report found over $15,000 in Section 1 subterranean damage along the rear foundation).
- *"Fascia board repainted and caulked during recent exterior refresh."* (Covering over active drywood kick-out holes and hollowed rafter tails).

## The Manual Review Problem: Overlooking Red Flags in Massive California Disclosure Packets

In a typical San Bernardino County transaction, buyers receive hundreds of pages during their standard **17-day Buyer Contingency Period**.

A complete buyer due diligence packet includes:
- The California Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)
- The Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD) Report detailing wildfire, seismic fault, and flood zones
- A 60-to-90-page General Home Inspection Report
- The SPCB Branch 3 WDO Pest Inspection Report (often with multiple supplemental re-inspection certifications)
- San Bernardino County and City building permit histories
- Preliminary Title Reports and CC&Rs

### Technical Caveats That Trap Inland Empire Homebuyers

Crucial red flags are often hidden within the fine print of inspection disclaimers:
- An inspector might note: *"Tile roof overhangs and low-pitch attic configuration prevented physical access to outer eaves and wall top-plates."* This caveat means the inspector never examined the exact zones where drywood termites routinely enter.
- A general home inspector might write: *"Stucco efflorescence and soil grading within 2 inches of weep screed at south elevation."* Unless the buyer cross-references this with the termite report, they will miss that high soil grade has bridged subterranean termites directly into the home's bottom wall plate.

Navigating dense technical documents under tight contingency timelines leaves buyers exposed to severe structural liabilities.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in San Bernardino

**1. Is a termite inspection required by law when buying a home in San Bernardino?**
California state law does not mandate a termite inspection for cash transactions. However, virtually all conventional, FHA, and VA mortgage lenders require a clear SPCB Branch 3 WDO report (specifically clearance of all Section 1 items) prior to funding the loan.

**2. What is the difference between Section 1 and Section 2 on a California WDO report?**
Section 1 items represent active wood-destroying pest infestations (such as live termites, beetles, or active fungus/dry rot) that require immediate chemical treatment, fumigation, or structural repair. Section 2 items represent conditions conducive to infestation (such as plumbing leaks, earth-to-wood contact, or poor crawlspace ventilation) that do not yet have active damage but should be corrected.

**3. Why are homes in San Bernardino susceptible to both drywood and subterranean termites?**
San Bernardino's climate features hot, dry summers and mild winters. Subterranean termites thrive in the valley's moist subsoils beneath foundations, while drywood termites swarm during warm autumn Santa Ana wind conditions, flying directly into attic eaves and vents without needing soil contact.

**4. Can a seller in San Bernardino sell a home "As-Is" to avoid termite disclosures?**
No. Under California Civil Code § 1102, an "As-Is" clause does not exempt a seller from delivering the Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) or Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ). The seller must still disclose all known material defects, including past or present termite infestations and previous structural treatments.
