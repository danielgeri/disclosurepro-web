Seeing a checked box next to "Past Termite Treatment" or "Prior Wood-Destroying Organism Infestation" on a seller property disclosure statement can instantly freeze a transaction—threatening prospective buyers with **$4,500 to over $45,000 in unbudgeted structural repairs**.

When **buying a house with a history of termites disclosure**, buyers frequently encounter two conflicting viewpoints: real estate agents who downplay the note as routine homeownership maintenance, and home inspectors who warn of compromised structural sill plates and hollowed wall framing. Termite activity is remarkably common across much of the United States, but past treatment does not automatically equal structural soundness. A disclosure noting prior treatment could signify a proactive homeowner who maintained an annual pest prevention contract—or a panicked seller who applied a localized chemical spot treatment to conceal catastrophic joist destruction weeks before putting the home on the market.

Before signing away your contractual inspection rights, understanding how to differentiate subterranean from drywood colonies, verify transferable termite bonds, demand historical Wood-Destroying Organism (WDO) records, and enforce your legal remedies is critical to protecting your investment.

## Evaluating Seller Disclosures: Decoding Past Treatment vs. Structural Damage

Every state requires residential sellers to complete property disclosure statements—such as the [California TDS and SPQ](/blog/california-tds-spq-disclosure-guide/) or the [Texas TREC Seller's Disclosure Notice](/blog/texas-trec-sellers-disclosure-guide/). These forms specifically probe for wood-destroying insect history.

When reviewing disclosure answers, buyers must distinguish between three distinct disclosure scenarios:

```
[ Scenario A: Preventative Bond ] --> Active Warranty, No Historical Framing Damage (Lowest Risk)
[ Scenario B: Treated Infestation ] --> Treatment Complete, Extent of Structural Damage Unknown (Requires Scope)
[ Scenario C: Cosmetic Patching ]   --> Evasive Disclosures, Concealed Soft Wood (Severe Legal/Financial Risk)
```

1. **Preventative Monitoring vs. Active Remediation:** A seller who checks "YES" because they installed an exterior baiting system when purchasing the home represents minimal risk. Conversely, a seller who checks "YES" for chemical treatment following a swarm inside the living room indicates that active insects were feeding directly on load-bearing structural members.
2. **"Treated and Resolved" Without Invoices:** A major red flag occurs when a seller writes: *"Treated for termites in 2022; no issues since,"* but fails to attach paid contractor invoices, treatment diagrams, or pesticide application records.
3. **The Unanswered Damage Follow-Up:** Most disclosure forms ask two sequential questions:
   - *Has the property ever been treated for termites or wood-destroying insects?*
   - *Are you aware of any unrepaired structural damage caused by termites or wood-destroying organisms?*
   Sellers routinely check "YES" to treatment but "NO" to damage. This response often relies on intentional ignorance: sellers assume that if an exterminator sprayed pesticide, the issue is closed—ignoring the reality that exterminators do not repair hollowed framing, sister rotted joists, or rebuild mudsills.

## Subterranean vs. Drywood Termites: Distinct Biology & Structural Threats

The structural implications of a past termite infestation depend entirely on the biological species involved. Conflating subterranean termites with drywood termites is one of the most dangerous mistakes prospective buyers make.

### 1. Subterranean Termites (Reticulitermes, Coptotermes)
Subterranean termites—including native species and the highly aggressive invasive Formosan termite—live in massive underground colonies consisting of several hundred thousand to several million insects:
- **Moisture & Soil Contact:** Subterranean colonies must maintain contact with soil moisture. They invade homes through hairline foundation cracks, crawlspace piers, and pipe penetrations, building protective mud tubes across concrete walls.
- **Structural Attack Pattern:** Subterranean termites feed on cellulose along the soft springwood grain, leaving a characteristic layered, honeycombed hollow inside sill plates, rim joists, and floor joists. Because they travel from the ground up, severe damage concentrates at foundation support members.

### 2. Drywood Termites (Incisitermes)
Drywood termites inhabit dry, sound wood entirely above ground without requiring soil contact:
- **Airborne Infestation:** Winged alates fly directly into attic vents, eave soffits, rafter tails, and window frames during warm autumn swarming seasons.
- **Structural Attack Pattern:** Drywood termites feed across the wood grain, hollowing out chambers in roof trusses, exterior trim, and door jambs. They push small, hexagonal fecal droppings (frass or "kick-out pellets") out of tiny pinholes.
- **Remediation Differences:** While subterranean termites require ground chemical barriers or baiting stations, widespread drywood infestations typically necessitate whole-structure tent fumigation (Vikane sulfuryl fluoride gas). Understanding these nuances is especially critical when evaluating [California Section 1 vs. Section 2 termite reports](/blog/section-1-vs-section-2-termite-report-california/), where active pests dictate mandatory escrow clearance.

## Verifying Termite Treatment Records & Transferable Termite Bonds

If a seller discloses past termite activity, buyers must insist on reviewing the complete historical documentation before removing their inspection contingency.

### Demanding Official WDO / NPMA-33 Reports
Never settle for an oral explanation. Demand the original pest management paperwork:
- **National Standard (NPMA-33):** Across most states, licensed exterminators document findings on the **National Pest Management Association NPMA-33 Wood-Destroying Insect Inspection Form**. Section IV details whether past evidence (mud tubes, damaged framing) was located and whether active live insects were observed.
- **Application Records & Chemicals:** Verify which chemical was utilized. Modern non-repellent soil termiticides like **Termidor SC (Fipronil)** or baiting systems like **Sentricon Always Active** provide long-lasting colony elimination. Older pyrethroid repellents (permethrin, bifenthrin) merely create temporary barriers that termites can bypass through soil gaps.

### The Fine Print of Transferable Termite Bonds
Many sellers tout that the home is covered by a "transferable termite bond." However, not all termite bonds provide equal protection:

| Termite Bond Type | What It Actually Covers | What It Leaves Uncovered | Buyer Risk Level |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **"Retreatment Only" Bond** | Pest company will return to re-spray or re-bait the perimeter if active termites re-emerge. | Company pays **$0.00** toward carpentry repairs, structural joist replacement, or damaged finish materials. | **HIGH**: Homeowner pays all structural repair costs out of pocket. |
| **"Retreatment & Repair" Bond** | Pest company covers chemical eradication AND pays for structural repairs up to a policy limit ($250k–$1M). | Pre-existing damage prior to contract date is strictly excluded; moisture-induced fungal rot is excluded. | **LOW TO MODERATE**: Excellent protection, provided pre-existing damage exclusions are vetted. |
| **Lapsed or Non-Transferable Contract** | Zero protection. Many pest warranties expire automatically upon title transfer unless a formal transfer fee is paid within 30 days. | Full liability falls on the buyer. | **CRITICAL**: Buyer must re-treat and pay initial setup fees. |

## Buyer Legal Rights & Remedies for Concealed Termite Damage

What happens if a buyer purchases a home where the seller marked "NO" to termite history, but during renovations three months after closing, a contractor pulls down drywall and uncovers hollowed, rotten framing?

### 1. Actual Knowledge and the Duty to Disclose
Under standard residential disclosure statutes across almost every state, sellers possess a legal duty to disclose known material latent defects. While sellers are generally not held liable for defects they genuinely did not know existed, they cannot hide behind plausible deniability when circumstantial evidence proves prior knowledge:
- **The Physical Paper Trail:** Exterminators maintain detailed customer logs. A buyer's attorney can subpoena local pest control companies to demonstrate that the previous owner received treatment estimates, termite inspections, or baiting services during their ownership.
- **Active Concealment:** Evidence such as spackle used to plug termite emergence pinholes, mud tubes painted over with modern latex paint, or fresh trim nailed directly over rotted band joists proves intentional, affirmative concealment.

### 2. Legal Causes of Action
When sellers fraudulently conceal active termite infestations or structural destruction, buyers can pursue multiple legal remedies:
- **Fraudulent Misrepresentation & Fraudulent Concealment:** Allows the buyer to recover compensatory damages (the full cost to eradicate the insects and structurally reconstruct the property).
- **Statutory Disclosure Violations:** Many state laws (such as Texas DTPA or Pennsylvania Seller Disclosure Law) provide statutory mechanisms for attorneys' fees and, in cases of willful fraud, treble damages.
- **Contractual Breach of Warranty:** If the purchase contract explicitly included a seller warranty of clear pest clearance that proved false.

### 3. Protecting Yourself Before Closing
Litigation is slow, expensive, and stressful. The most effective protection is exercising your contractual due diligence rights before closing escrow:
1. **Never Waive the Pest Contingency:** Even in aggressive seller markets, preserving an independent WDO inspection contingency protects your deposit.
2. **Hire an Independent Structural Engineer:** If an inspector identifies historical termite damage, immediately hire a licensed structural engineer (not a real estate agent's preferred handyman) to measure deflection and verify whether joist capacity is compromised.
3. **Draft an Enforceable Repair Credit Addendum:** If structural sistering or treatment is needed, do not allow the seller to select the contractor. ## Frequently Asked Questions About Buying a House With a Termite History

### Is buying a house with a history of termites safe?
Yes, buying a home with a documented history of termites can be completely safe—provided the infestation was fully eradicated and any damaged structural framing was properly repaired or reinforced by a licensed contractor. What matters is not whether the home had termites in the past, but whether the structural integrity of the mudsills, floor joists, and studs remains intact and whether an active termite prevention warranty is in place.

### Can termites cause severe structural damage in just one year?
While an individual termite is tiny, a mature subterranean termite colony containing 500,000 to over two million workers can consume up to a pound of wood per day. If an undetected colony enters an older home with preexisting moisture issues, it can hollow out an entire load-bearing stud, subfloor section, or rim joist within 6 to 12 months, causing visible floor sag and costly structural failure in a single year.

### What is the difference between a termite treatment receipt and a termite bond?
A termite treatment receipt simply proves that a pest control operator applied chemicals or installed baiting stations on a specific date in the past. A termite bond is an ongoing contractual warranty with a pest management company that includes annual re-inspections and promises free retreatment—and sometimes structural repair coverage—if termites return during the active contract period.

### What legal rights do I have if a seller concealed active termite damage on the disclosure?
If a seller knowingly concealed active termite damage or prior treatments—such as by painting over mud tubes, plugging emergence holes with wood filler, or falsely answering "NO" on a statutory disclosure form—the buyer can bring legal action for fraudulent misrepresentation, breach of contract, and violation of state disclosure statutes. Prevailing buyers may recover the full cost of extermination, structural repairs, and reasonable attorney fees.
