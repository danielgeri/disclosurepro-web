## What Is a CLUE Report and How Does LexisNexis Track Property Loss History?

The **C.L.U.E. Personal Property report** is a comprehensive 7-year claims database managed by consumer reporting agency LexisNexis. An estimated **99% of property and casualty insurers** in the United States report all claim activities directly into this clearinghouse.

A standard C.L.U.E. report details every claim associated with a specific parcel of real estate over the preceding seven years:
- **Date of Loss:** The exact calendar day the incident occurred.
- **Type of Loss:** Categorized by peril—such as Water (sudden and accidental discharge), Wind/Hail, Fire/Smoke, Theft, Vandalism, or Liability (dog bite/slip-and-fall).
- **Insurance Carrier Name & Policy Number:** The underwriter that held the risk at the time of the event.
- **Claim Status & Amount Paid:** Distinguishes between claims paid in full, claims denied under policy exclusions, and claims closed with zero payout.

### The Fundamental Property Risk Rule: Claims Follow the House
The most critical reality that blindsides homebuyers is that **insurance claims attach to the real estate, not just the individual policyholder**. 

Even if you have maintained a pristine, claims-free 20-year driving and homeownership record, purchasing a property with two prior water damage claims will trigger underwriting penalties against *your* new policy. Underwriters view a property with multiple historical claims as structurally prone to repeated losses—whether due to faulty subsurface plumbing, deteriorating roof decking, or poor site grading.

## The Trap of the "Zero-Dollar" Inquiry Claim

Many homeowners do not realize that simply calling their insurance company to inquire about coverage can create a permanent mark on the property's record.

If a seller noticed water pooling under an upstairs bathroom vanity and called their insurance agent to ask, *"If a pipe bursts, does my policy cover the subfloor?"*, the carrier's customer service representative often logs a formal claim record. Even if the homeowner decided not to file a claim, paid for the repair out of pocket, and the carrier paid **$0.00**, LexisNexis records the entry as:
- **Peril:** Water Damage
- **Status:** Closed / Withdrawn
- **Amount Paid:** $0

When you apply for homeowner's insurance during escrow, underwriting algorithms scan the C.L.U.E. database. The algorithm flags a "Water Loss" event at the address, prompting the automated underwriting engine to apply a risk surcharge or require an intrusive physical 4-Point Inspection before binding coverage.

## Why Sellers Omit Past Insurance Claims From Statutory Disclosures

Why is there such a massive discrepancy between seller disclosure forms and actual C.L.U.E. reports? The reasons fall into three distinct patterns:

1. **The "Repaired Means Non-Material" Myth:** Sellers mistakenly believe that if an insurance carrier paid $18,000 to dry out a flooded kitchen and install new cabinets, the past water damage is no longer a "material defect." However, statutory disclosure laws require disclosure of past water intrusion regardless of remediation status.
2. **Pocketing the Payout (The Cosmetic Cover-Up):** In storm-damaged or older homes, sellers often submit roof or plumbing claims, receive a $15,000 insurance settlement check, and spend only $2,000 on cosmetic patching—pocketing the remaining cash. Because no licensed restoration firm completed the structural repairs, latent mold or rotted framing remains sealed behind fresh drywall and paint.
3. **Intentional Fraud to Avoid Price Reductions:** Listing agents and sellers recognize that advertising a history of plumbing slab leaks or mold remediation depresses property value. Sellers take their chances on standard buyer due diligence, knowing that general home inspectors cannot see inside closed plumbing lines or behind wall insulation.

## Real-World Case Study: The Hidden $24,000 Slab Leak Claim in Tampa, FL

In October 2026, a homebuyer entered escrow on a 1993 single-story block home in Tampa, Florida, for $515,000. On the Florida Realtors Seller's Property Disclosure, the seller marked "No" to any known past or present plumbing leaks, pipe ruptures, or flood damage.

During the contractual 12-day inspection period, the buyer engaged their insurance broker to secure a standard homeowner's hazard policy. The broker pulled the LexisNexis C.L.U.E. report for the parcel, which immediately flagged a **$24,150 paid water damage claim** from November 2024. The loss details noted an underground copper pinhole leak beneath the kitchen foundation slab that flooded living room flooring and kitchen cabinetry.

Armed with this discovery, the buyer executed a targeted due diligence investigation:
1. **Specialized Inspection:** The buyer hired an independent home inspector equipped with high-resolution infrared thermal imaging. The inspector identified elevated moisture readings along the rear kitchen drywall and discovered that the seller had merely bypassed the single leaking pipe with an exposed flex line behind the cabinets, leaving original corroding copper under the remainder of the slab.
2. **Missing Remediation Certification:** When the buyer demanded documentation, the seller admitted they handled drywall patching and cabinet installation themselves to save on the insurance deductible, with no professional IICRC dry-out certification.
3. **Insurance Underwriting Freeze:** The buyer's insurance carrier refused to bind a standard policy without proof of a licensed whole-house re-pipe, threatening the buyer's mortgage loan approval.

The buyer's agent submitted a structured [formal repair credit addendum](/blog/how-to-write-repair-credit-addendum-after-inspection/) demanding a **$14,000 closing cost credit** to cover an Uponor PEX-A whole-house re-pipe and professional mold testing, alongside an amended seller disclosure. Because the listing agent realized that canceling the sale would require disclosing the uninsurable slab leak to every subsequent buyer, the seller accepted the full **$14,000 credit**.

## How Homebuyers Legally Obtain a C.L.U.E. Report

Under the federal **Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)**, LexisNexis treats property loss histories as proprietary consumer reports. This means **only the current property owner (the seller) can directly pull an official C.L.U.E. Home Seller's Disclosure Report**. Prospective buyers cannot order one directly from LexisNexis without owner authorization.

However, buyers have two proven methods to obtain this critical data during due diligence:

### Method 1: The Contractual Purchase Agreement Clause
Incorporate an express C.L.U.E. report contingency into your purchase offer:
> *"Within three (3) business days of mutual acceptance, Seller shall order and deliver to Buyer a current LexisNexis C.L.U.E. Home Seller's Disclosure Report covering the preceding five (5) to seven (7) years. Buyer's approval of said report is a condition of this Agreement."*

Sellers can order their report free of charge once every 12 months under federal law via the LexisNexis Consumer Portal.

### Method 2: The Insurance Broker Inquiry (The "Workaround")
The moment your offer is accepted, contact your homeowner's insurance broker and request a full property insurance quote. When the underwriter runs the property address through their automated underwriting database, they will review the C.L.U.E. loss history to generate your premium rate. 

Ask your broker directly: *"Did the automated underwriting run flag any prior claims, water losses, or structural inquiries on this property within the last seven years?"* Your broker can disclose whether claims exist and explain how they will impact your annual insurance premium.

## Actionable Steps: What to Do When a C.L.U.E. Report Flags Prior Claims

If your C.L.U.E. report or insurance broker reveals past claims that were omitted from seller disclosures:

1. **Demand the Complete Claim File:** Request copies of the original insurance adjuster's summary, the scope of work, and paid contractor invoices proving repairs were completed by licensed professionals.
2. **Require Proof of Mold Remediation:** For any water damage claim exceeding $5,000, demand an **IICRC Certificate of Completion** and passing post-remediation air quality clearance testing.
3. **Leverage the Omission in Repair Negotiations:** An uninsurable condition is the ultimate negotiation leverage. Use the insurance underwriter's formal premium surcharge or declination letter to demand a closing credit, price reduction, or whole-system replacement before waiving your inspection contingency.

## Frequently Asked Questions About CLUE Reports & Home Buying

### How far back does a C.L.U.E. report show property insurance claims?
A standard LexisNexis C.L.U.E. Personal Property report maintains a rolling **7-year record** of insurance claims and inquiries. Claims older than seven years automatically roll off the report and are generally not factored into standard automated underwriting decisions.

### Can I pull a C.L.U.E. report on a house I don't own yet?
No. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA), only the current property owner or an authorized insurance carrier evaluating a policy application can access the report. Buyers must either require the seller to furnish a copy as part of the purchase contract or have their insurance agent review property claims history during the insurance quote process.

### Does a denied claim or $0 inquiry show up on a C.L.U.E. report?
Yes. Any contact with an insurance claims department that generates a claim file number is logged into the C.L.U.E. database, even if the claim was denied under a policy exclusion or closed without any payment. Underwriters may still treat zero-dollar water inquiries as evidence of an underlying maintenance problem.

### What should I do if the seller refused to disclose a major claim found on the C.L.U.E. report?
Treat it as a major red flag. Review the claim details with your home inspector and contractor to verify whether proper structural repairs were completed. If the seller intentionally concealed a material defect, you have strong grounds to demand a significant repair credit, require licensed contractor warranties, or exercise your inspection contingency to terminate the transaction and receive a full return of your earnest money deposit.
