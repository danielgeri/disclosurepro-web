While most homebuyers obsess over home inspection reports, overlooking the fine print of a **Preliminary Title Report (Title Commitment)** can burden you with an inherited **$8,000 to $45,000+ legal and financial liability** before you even receive your front-door keys. 

When escrow opens, the title company issues a preliminary report detailing the conditions under which it will insure your ownership. While sellers fill out standard condition disclosures like the [Texas TREC Seller's Disclosure Notice (§ 5.008)](/blog/texas-trec-sellers-disclosure-guide/) or the [California Seller Property Questionnaire (C.A.R. Form SPQ)](/blog/california-tds-spq-disclosure-guide/), they routinely omit title defects, believing the title officer will "handle everything at closing."

However, standard title insurance policies contain a critical caveat: **Schedule B, Section II Exceptions**. 

If a mechanic's lien, blanket utility easement, solar fixture filing, or restrictive covenant is listed as an exception on Schedule B, the title insurance company will **not** pay a single dollar if that cloud impairs your property rights or triggers foreclosure. 

Understanding the most dangerous [preliminary title report red flags homebuyers](/blog/5-disclosure-red-flags/) encounter is vital to safeguarding your equity during the due diligence period.

## Anatomy of a Title Commitment: Schedule A vs. Schedule B

A preliminary title report is not a guarantee of clear title; it is an offer to issue a title insurance policy subject to stated exclusions, conditions, and exceptions. To analyze a prelim effectively, buyers must distinguish between its two primary sections:

### Schedule A: The Property Profile & Transaction Terms
Schedule A outlines the administrative parameters of the transaction:
- **The Effective Date:** The exact date and time through which county public records were searched. Any encumbrance recorded after this timestamp will not appear until a final title update.
- **The Policy Amount:** Typically matches the agreed-upon purchase price.
- **Legal Description:** The metes-and-bounds or lot-and-block legal description. Always compare this against your property survey to ensure you are buying the exact parcel depicted on the plat map.
- **Vesting:** Identifies the current legal owner of record. If the seller listed on the purchase agreement does not match the vested owner on Schedule A (common in probate, divorce, or corporate LLC sales), escrow cannot close until proper signing authority is established.

### Schedule B: Requirements vs. Exceptions
Schedule B is divided into two distinct subsections:
1. **Schedule B-I (Requirements):** Matters that must be resolved prior to closing—such as paying off the seller's existing mortgage, submitting death certificates, or satisfying outstanding property tax judgments.
2. **Schedule B-II (Exceptions from Coverage):** The crucial list of encumbrances, easements, and restrictions that the title company **refuses to insure**. If a dispute arises over an item listed in Schedule B-II, your title policy will provide zero legal defense or financial compensation.

## The 6 Most Critical Schedule B Exceptions Buyers Overlook

Escrow officers and real estate agents often treat Schedule B exceptions as routine boilerplate. In reality, buried within those numbered paragraphs are severe encumbrances that can restrict property use, prevent renovations, or result in surprise cash liabilities.

### 1. Unreleased Mechanics Liens
If the seller recently remodeled a kitchen, installed a HVAC unit, or replaced a roof, any general contractor, subcontractor, or building materials supplier who was not fully paid can record a **mechanic's lien** against the property. Under state statutes (such as Texas Property Code Chapter 53 or California Civil Code § 8400), mechanics liens can retain legal priority over your new purchase mortgage. If an unreleased mechanic's lien appears on Schedule B, the buyer must require the seller to pay the claim and record a formal Release of Lien before escrow closes.

### 2. Leased Solar Panels & UCC-1 Financing Statements
When a homeowner leases solar panels or finances a photovoltaic system via a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA), the solar company records a **UCC-1 Financing Statement** with the county recorder. This document establishes a legal security interest in the solar equipment mounted to the roof. If the buyer does not officially assume the 20-year lease agreement or if the seller fails to buy out the system, the solar lender can place a cloud on the title, freezing loan funding.

### 3. Blanket and Pipeline Utility Easements
While standard perimeter utility easements (such as a 5-foot setback along the rear property line for power lines) are normal, a **blanket easement** grants a utility provider or pipeline company unrestricted access across the *entire* parcel. If an easement does not specify an exact metes-and-bounds path, building an in-ground swimming pool, garage, or detached ADU could result in the utility company demanding immediate demolition of the structure without compensation.

### 4. Shared Driveways & Ingress/Egress Agreements
Properties accessed via shared private roads or mutual driveways must have an express, recorded **Ingress/Egress Easement and Maintenance Agreement**. If Schedule B notes a shared access easement without an accompanying maintenance covenant, you may be held jointly liable for thousands of dollars in private road repaving or face hostile disputes if a neighbor blocks road access.

### 5. Delinquent HOA Liens & Super-Lien Priority
In planned unit developments (PUDs) and condominium communities, unpaid monthly homeowner association dues or unpaid special assessments become an automatic assessment lien on the property. In more than 20 "super-lien" states, delinquent HOA liens take priority over the first mortgage, meaning the buyer could be forced to pay thousands in back assessments, late fees, and legal charges upon taking ownership.

### 6. Restrictive CC&R Covenants
Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions (CC&Rs) dictate how you may use your property. Standard title reports list the book-and-page recording references for CC&Rs. Crucial restrictions frequently hidden inside these documents include:
- Strict prohibitions on short-term rentals (Airbnb/VRBO) or long-term leasing caps.
- Bans on commercial vehicle parking, boats, RVs, or utility trailers in driveways.
- Architectural review committee (ARC) veto power over paint colors, fencing styles, and solar panel placement.

## Standard CLTA vs. ALTA Homeowner's Policy: Why Extended Coverage Matters

When closing on a home, buyers are typically offered two primary types of title insurance: a **Standard Coverage Policy** (often CLTA in Western states) and an **ALTA Homeowner's Policy (Extended Coverage)**.

### The Limitations of Standard Coverage
Standard title insurance policies only cover defects that are recorded in public land records. They explicitly exclude:
- Unrecorded mechanics liens or construction liabilities.
- Boundary line disputes, encroachments, or unrecorded easements that an accurate boundary survey would reveal.
- Post-closing violations of subdivision or building permit ordinances.

### Why You Should Demand ALTA Extended Coverage
An **ALTA Homeowner's Policy** provides substantially enhanced protection for residential buyers of 1-to-4 family properties. For a modest additional premium (typically $150 to $350 extra at closing), extended coverage protects against:
- Forced removal of existing structures because they encroach onto an easement or across a neighbor's property line.
- Unrecorded mechanics liens for work performed prior to closing.
- Post-policy forgery, fraud, or adverse possession claims.
- Supplemental property taxes assessed for construction completed before your purchase date.

## Real-World Case Study: The Surprise $22,400 Roofer's Mechanic's Lien in Austin, TX

In October 2026, a homebuyer went under contract on a $680,000 remodeled home in North Austin, Texas. The MLS listing proudly highlighted a "Brand New Architectural Shingle Roof Installed June 2026." The seller's disclosure confirmed the roof replacement and checked "No" to any known liens or encumbrances.

Seven business days before scheduled closing, the title company delivered the preliminary title report. While Schedule A verified legal vesting, **Schedule B, Item 14** listed an active **Affidavit Claiming Mechanic's Lien** in the amount of **$22,400**, recorded by an Austin commercial building materials supplier under Texas Property Code Chapter 53.

The buyer's research team uncovered the chain of events:
1. The seller had paid the general contractor in full for the roof.
2. However, the general contractor absconded without paying the roofing supply wholesaler for the shingles and underlayment materials.
3. Because Texas law allows material suppliers to file liens against real property within statutory notice windows, the supplier attached the $22,400 lien directly to the home's title.

The buyer took immediate tactical action:
- Invoking the contract's **Title Contingency Clause**, the buyer issued a formal **Title Objection Letter** demanding that the cloud be cleared.
- The buyer's agent submitted a structured [formal repair credit and escrow holdback addendum](/blog/how-to-write-repair-credit-addendum-after-inspection/), refusing to close until the title company received an unconditional lien release.
- Facing a canceled sale, the seller instructed the escrow officer to hold $25,000 from their net proceeds in an escrow indemnity account to pay off the supplier, secure a recorded Release of Lien, and deliver free and clear title to the buyer.

## How to Formally Object to Preliminary Title Exceptions

Do not assume the title company will fix Schedule B exceptions automatically. Follow these due diligence steps immediately upon receiving your prelim:

1. **Calculate Your Title Review Window:** Most standard residential purchase agreements grant the buyer **5 to 7 calendar days** from receipt of the preliminary report to review documents and submit formal written objections.
2. **Request Copies of All Underlying Exception Documents:** The preliminary report merely lists recording numbers (e.g., "Doc #2022-094821"). Require your title officer to provide digital copies of every underlying easement, covenant, and agreement referenced in Schedule B.
3. **Cross-Check Against Seller Disclosures and HOA Records:** Compare Schedule B exceptions against your [HOA reserve study and assessment disclosures](/blog/hoa-reserve-study-special-assessment-red-flags/). If the title report notes an HOA assessment lien that does not appear on the seller's property disclosure, you have immediate leverage to demand seller clearance.
4. **Deliver a Written Title Objection Notice:** If an exception threatens your intended property use, submit a written objection letter requiring the seller to eliminate the defect at their sole expense before closing. If the seller cannot or will not remove the exception, you retain the legal right to terminate the contract and receive a full refund of your earnest money deposit.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Preliminary Title Reports

### What is the difference between a title search and title insurance?
A title search is the investigative process of reviewing public records, deeds, mortgages, court judgments, and tax rolls to identify existing clouds and encumbrances. Title insurance is the indemnity policy purchased at closing that legally defends your ownership and compensates you financially if an unexcepted title defect, forgery, or recording error surfaces after closing.

### Can I cancel a home purchase contract based on the preliminary title report?
Yes. Standard residential purchase contracts include a title contingency granting buyers the right to review the preliminary report and underlying exception documents. If the report reveals an objectionable encumbrance (such as a restrictive easement, mechanic's lien, or boundary dispute) that the seller refuses to cure or bond around, the buyer can terminate the contract and recover their earnest money deposit.

### Who pays for the owner's title insurance policy?
Custom varies by state and county. In Southern California, Florida, and parts of Texas, the seller traditionally pays for the owner's policy, while the buyer pays for the lender's title policy. In Northern California and parts of the Pacific Northwest, the buyer and seller often split the cost equally. Regardless of who pays, the buyer has the legal right under federal RESPA (Section 9) to select the title and escrow provider.

### How does a solar panel UCC-1 filing affect my title?
A UCC-1 fixture filing notifies creditors that the solar panels on the roof are personal property owned by a third-party leasing company, not real property attached to the home. If you are obtaining a mortgage, your lender's underwriter will require the solar company to temporarily subordinate their UCC-1 lien or require the seller to pay off the balance before your mortgage can record in first-lien position.
