Missing a buried clue on Pennsylvania's **Seller's Property Disclosure Statement (PAR Form SPD)**—such as Drylok-painted basement seepage, unmitigated indoor radon, coal mine subsidence exposure, or trapped moisture behind Southeastern PA stucco—regularly hits buyers with **$10,000 to $95,000+ in post-settlement repair bills**. Under the **Pennsylvania Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law (RESDL), 68 Pa.C.S. §§ 7301–7315**, sellers of 1-to-4 unit residential properties must disclose all known material defects in writing *before* you sign the **PAR Standard Agreement for the Sale of Real Estate (Form ASR)**.

Yet across Philadelphia rowhomes, Main Line colonials, Lehigh Valley subdivisions, and Pittsburgh hillsides, sellers routinely check "Unknown" on technicalities or soften chronic water and structural issues behind polite one-line notes. Because RESDL imposes a strict **2-year statute of limitations**—and winning a post-closing nondisclosure lawsuit requires proving the seller's *actual knowledge*—your real financial protection happens during your **10-to-15-day Form ASR inspection contingency window**, before closing locks in the liability.

## Understanding 68 Pa.C.S. RESDL: Legal Duties, Exemptions & the 2-Year Clock

The **Pennsylvania Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law (68 Pa.C.S. §§ 7301–7315)** sets the statutory baseline reflected across the 11-page **Pennsylvania Association of Realtors Seller's Property Disclosure Statement (PAR Form SPD)**.

### Four Statutory Rules Every Pennsylvania Homebuyer Must Know
1. **Mandatory Pre-Contract Delivery (§ 7303):** Sellers must deliver the completed written disclosure before the buyer executes the Agreement of Sale. Selling a Pennsylvania home **"As-Is"** under PAR Form ASR waives the seller's obligation to perform repairs—it does **not** exempt the seller from completing PAR Form SPD truthfully.
2. **Actual Knowledge Standard (§ 7308):** Sellers are legally obligated to disclose only known **material defects** (problems having a significant adverse impact on property value or posing an unreasonable risk to occupant safety). Sellers are not required to hire inspectors or conduct independent investigations before signing—meaning a clean "No" or "Unknown" never substitutes for specialized testing.
3. **Ongoing Duty to Supplement Before Settlement (§ 7307):** If an answer on PAR Form SPD becomes materially inaccurate between contract signing and settlement—for example, a heavy storm floods the basement or a furnace heat exchanger cracks during escrow—the seller must promptly deliver a written update.
4. **Strict 2-Year Statute of Limitations (§ 7311) & Narrow Exemptions (§ 7302):** Under **68 Pa.C.S. § 7311**, buyers have just **two years after the date of final settlement** to file a civil action for damages resulting from a willful or negligent RESDL violation. Meanwhile, **§ 7302** exempts certain transfers—including estate executors/administrators who never occupied the home, foreclosure/REO lenders, and transfers pursuant to court order—making invasive inspections essential on estate sales and investor flips.

## High-Risk Sections of PAR Form SPD Every Buyer Must Audit

Rather than skimming 21 sections of PAR Form SPD at the same speed, concentrate your inspection budget and cross-checking on the seven defect categories that trigger the largest Pennsylvania repair bills:

### 1. Roof, Attic & Ice-Dam Leaks (Section 5)
Pennsylvania's freeze-thaw winters punish aging asphalt shingles, historic slate roofs, and flat EPDM rubber roofs on Philadelphia and Pittsburgh rowhomes. Watch for sellers who mark "No" to active leaks while admitting *"repaired flashing around chimney in 2023"*—just like the Northeast moisture failures detailed in our [Boston, MA roof inspection and disclosure guide](/blog/boston-ma-roofs/), roofers often patch surface flashing while leaving saturated attic decking and moldy rafters hidden underneath.

### 2. Basements, Crawlspaces & Sump Systems (Section 6)
Hydrostatic pressure in Pennsylvania clay and shale soils makes basement water intrusion the #1 source of PA disclosure disputes. Scrutiny points include:
- **Euphemisms:** *"Dampness during heavy storms,"* *"dehumidifier runs in summer,"* or *"installed second sump pump as precaution."*
- **Visual Concealment:** Freshly applied white waterproof masonry paint (Drylok), brand-new basement carpet, or recently drywalled perimeter walls directly over stone or cinderblock foundations frequently mask chronic seepage, efflorescence, and bowed block walls.

### 3. Termites / Wood-Destroying Insects & Foundation Framing (Sections 7–8)
Older stone and brick foundations throughout Southeastern and Western PA harbor **Eastern Subterranean Termites** inside damp masonry joist pockets—identical to the historic timber-sill risks analyzed in our [Buffalo, NY termite disclosure guide](/blog/buffalo-ny-termites/). Always pair Section 7 disclosures with an independent **NPMA-33 WDI inspection**, especially when finished basement ceilings obstruct sill plates.

### 4. Sewage, Municipal Dye Tests & On-Lot Septic Systems (Sections 11–12)
In older suburbs around Pittsburgh (Allegheny County) and Philadelphia, municipal **point-of-sale sewer dye tests** and video lateral inspections routinely uncover illegal storm-drain cross-connections or crushed terra-cotta sewer pipes. In rural and exurban PA counties (Chester, Lancaster, Bucks, Butler, Monroe), never rely on a simple septic pumping receipt—order a full **PSMA (Pennsylvania Septage Management Association)** hydraulic load and absorption-area inspection to verify the leach field or sand mound is not failing.

### 5. Pennsylvania's Extreme Radon Risk (Section 18)
Pennsylvania sits across uranium-bearing limestone, shale, and the **Reading Prong** geological formation, resulting in roughly **40% of Pennsylvania homes testing above the EPA 4.0 pCi/L action threshold**. Even if PAR Form SPD discloses a clean radon test from six years ago, seasonal stack effect, foundation settling, and new HVAC installations change indoor radon readings dramatically. Always run an independent 48-hour continuous radon monitor during your inspection period.

### 6. Coal Severance, Mine Subsidence & Karst Sinkholes (Sections 17 & 20)
Pennsylvania has a century-long coal mining legacy across Western PA (bituminous coal belt around Pittsburgh, Washington, and Westmoreland counties) and Northeastern PA (anthracite belt around Scranton and Wilkes-Barre), alongside **karst limestone sinkholes** in Central Pennsylvania and the Lehigh Valley.
- Verify the mandatory **Pennsylvania Coal Notice (52 P.S. § 1551)** inside your Agreement of Sale warning whether subsurface coal rights were severed without surface-support rights.
- Check the property address against the **PA Department of Environmental Protection (PA DEP) Mine Subsidence Insurance (MSI)** underground-mine mapping tool during due diligence, because standard homeowner policies exclude mine subsidence collapse completely.

### 7. The Southeastern PA Stucco & EIFS Moisture Crisis
In **Chester, Montgomery, Bucks, Delaware, and Lancaster counties**, thousands of residential homes built between 1990 and 2012 with hard-coat stucco or barrier EIFS suffer catastrophic hidden water intrusion around windows, kick-out roof flashing, and ledger boards. Because the exterior stucco coat often looks pristine while underlying OSB sheathing and wall studs rot away, sellers routinely check "No" to exterior wall defects in complete good faith. On **any** stucco-clad PA home, add a dedicated **Stucco Inspection Contingency** allowing an **invasive moisture-meter probe inspection** before releasing contingencies.

- **Credit vs. Seller Repairs:** In Pennsylvania, requesting a **lender-permitted seller assist (closing cost credit)** or **purchase price reduction** almost always beats letting a departing seller hire the cheapest contractor to patch a basement wall or flat roof.

Confirm in PAR Form ASR whether the seller is paying U&O repair costs or attempting to shift them onto you at settlement.

## Frequently Asked Questions About the Pennsylvania Seller's Property Disclosure Statement

### Does selling a house "As-Is" in Pennsylvania exempt the seller from completing PAR Form SPD?
No. Under the **Pennsylvania Real Estate Seller Disclosure Law (68 Pa.C.S. §§ 7301–7315)**, an "As-Is" clause in the Agreement of Sale only means the seller will not make repairs or offer credits after your inspections. Unless the transaction qualifies for a narrow statutory exemption under § 7302 (such as certain estate/fiduciary transfers or foreclosures), the seller is still legally required to deliver a truthful, completed Seller's Property Disclosure Statement before you sign the contract.

### How long do I have to sue if a Pennsylvania seller lied on the disclosure form?
Under **68 Pa.C.S. § 7311**, a buyer must file a RESDL civil lawsuit within **two years from the date of final settlement**. However, proving that a seller had *actual prior knowledge* of a concealed defect—and recovering legal fees—is expensive and uncertain. Catching omissions during your 10-to-15-day Form ASR inspection period and demanding a pre-closing credit is dramatically safer and faster.

### Why is an invasive stucco inspection recommended in Pennsylvania even if PAR Form SPD says "No Defects"?
Hard-coat stucco and EIFS moisture damage in Southeastern Pennsylvania (Chester, Montgomery, Bucks, and Delaware counties) occurs silently *behind* the exterior masonry shell, rotting OSB sheathing and wall studs long before interior drywall stains appear. Because a seller can honestly mark "No" without ever realizing their walls are wet inside, an invasive probe inspection during your contingency window is the only reliable way to avoid a $40,000 to $100,000+ remediation bill.

### Are Pennsylvania sellers required to disclose radon levels or mine subsidence risk?
Yes—if they have actual knowledge. PAR Form SPD includes dedicated questions requiring sellers to disclose any known prior radon test results, installed mitigation systems, underground mine subsidence damage, coal-rights severances, or sinkholes. Because roughly 40% of PA homes exceed the EPA's 4.0 pCi/L radon threshold and historic coal mines underlie huge swaths of Western and Northeastern PA, buyers should always pair the SPD with a fresh 48-hour radon test and a PA DEP Mine Subsidence Insurance map check.
