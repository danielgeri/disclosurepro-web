## The Hidden Threat: Subterranean and Drywood Termites in Rancho Cucamonga

Rancho Cucamonga sits on the massive alluvial fan spreading south from Cucamonga Peak and the San Gabriel Mountain range. Our local climate features high summer temperatures regularly exceeding 100°F, low ambient humidity, and powerful autumn Santa Ana wind gusts originating from the high desert through the Cajon Pass. These geological and atmospheric conditions create an intense habitat for multiple wood-destroying pest species.

### Why Inland Empire Foothill Housing Stock Elevates Pest Risk

Several unique regional construction practices, foothill wind dynamics, and landscaping factors accelerate termite activity across Rancho Cucamonga:

- **The Cajon Pass Wind Corridor and Drywood Swarming Seasons:** Rancho Cucamonga is located directly in the path of ferocious autumn Santa Ana winds. During late summer and autumn heatwaves, drywood termite reproductive alates take flight in massive swarms. High winds disperse them across neighborhoods, where they enter unmeshed attic gable vents, open rafter tails, and decorative wooden patio covers.
- **Slab-on-Grade Foundations and Plumbing Knockouts:** Most homes in Rancho Cucamonga built between the 1970s and 2000s sit on monolithic concrete slabs. Concrete naturally shrinks and cracks over decades. Subterranean termites require an opening only 1/32nd of an inch wide to enter through plumbing rough-ins beneath master bathroom Roman tubs, kitchen islands, and laundry rooms.
- **Buried Stucco Weep Screeds and Planter Beds:** In foothill neighborhoods, exterior stucco finishes frequently terminate below finished grade, buried under decorative bark mulch, flagstone walkways, or raised planter beds. When the metal weep screed is obscured, termites construct mud shelter tubes directly behind the stucco into exterior wall framing without leaving visible traces on exterior walls.
- **Architectural Vulnerabilities Across Rancho Cucamonga Neighborhoods:**
  - *Alta Loma:* 1970s to 1990s custom and semi-custom ranch homes featuring extensive exposed wood fascia, heavy timber patio structures, and mature shade trees. Drywood termites frequently nest inside exposed eave beams and attic trusses.
  - *Etiwanda:* Blend of historic early-20th-century stone cottages and modern master-planned communities. Settling cold joints between concrete patio slabs and home foundations provide direct subterranean pathways into bottom sill plates.
  - *Victoria and Red Hill:* 1980s to 2000s two-story stucco homes with concrete tile roofs. Tile roofs trap heat during summer months, driving drywood colonies deep into ceiling joists and exterior wall headers.

### Pest Biology: Subterranean vs. Drywood Termites

Rancho Cucamonga properties face simultaneous pressure from two distinct wood-destroying insect species:
- **Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** Ground-nesting pests that require soil moisture. They construct tubular mud shelter tunnels across foundation concrete stem walls to reach structural wood framing, feeding on soft springwood along the grain.
- **Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*):** Entirely wood-dwelling organisms that do not require soil contact or external moisture. They enter directly into exposed roof eaves, fascia boards, and window headers, excavating smooth galleries across the wood grain and pushing distinctive six-sided fecal pellets (frass) out through tiny kickout holes.

## California Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Statutory Framework and Seller Obligations

California provides comprehensive statutory disclosure requirements designed to protect residential real estate purchasers from hidden material property defects.

### California Civil Code § 1102: Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and SPQ

Under **California Civil Code § 1102 et seq.**, sellers of residential real property must execute and deliver the statutory **Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)** alongside the California Association of REALTORS® (C.A.R.) **Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)**:

- **Section II, Part C of the TDS:** Requires the seller to disclose whether they are aware of any active pest infestations, wood-destroying insect damage, or previous structural treatments.
- **Section V of the SPQ (Pests, Infestations & Wood Rot):** Mandates specific disclosures regarding:
  - Any known past or present infestations of termites, dry rot, beetles, or wood-destroying organisms.
  - Any prior pest control inspection reports, remediation contracts, or repair estimates.
  - The existence of any active termite warranty, treatment bond, or transferable service contract.

### California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) Reports: Section 1 vs. Section 2

In California, pest inspections must be performed by a Branch 3 licensed structural pest inspector governed by the **California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB)**. The inspection report is divided into two distinct sections:
- **Section 1 Items:** Conditions with active infestations, active dry rot infections, or visible structural wood damage. Lenders frequently mandate complete Section 1 clearance before funding residential purchase mortgages.
- **Section 2 Items:** Conditions deemed conducive to future infestations (such as earth-to-wood contact, plumbing leaks, buried stucco weep screeds, or inadequate subfloor ventilation).
- **Inaccessible Areas:** The inspector explicitly records zones that were obstructed from visual inspection, such as finished garage drywall, sub-slab plumbing lines, locked storage units, or fully insulated attics.

### Legal Liability for Non-Disclosure in California

Under California legal precedents established in landmark decisions like *Easton v. Strassburger* (1984) and *Shapiro v. Sutherland* (1998), sellers and listing agents have an affirmative legal duty to disclose all known material facts that affect the value and desirability of the property.

An "As-Is" provision in a California purchase contract does not shield a seller from liability for affirmative fraudulent concealment or intentional omission of known structural pest defects.

### What Rancho Cucamonga Sellers Reveal vs. Conceal

Sellers in San Bernardino County frequently attempt to downplay historical pest issues on disclosure documents:
- *"Local spot treatment performed on patio cover in 2022."* (Conceals that drywood termite frass was discovered falling inside attic insulation).
- *"Minor drywall patch in laundry room due to settling."* (Hides the fact that structural studs had been hollowed out by subterranean termites).
- *"Section 1 clearance issued by seller's exterminator."* (Fails to disclose that the contractor performed only localized chemical foaming rather than a recommended whole-house tent fumigation).

### The Risk of Disconnected Clues

Material termite hazards rarely appear as an explicit, single warning in real estate closing packets. Instead, critical indicators are fragmented across independent reports:
- The Home Inspector writes on page 41: *"Exterior stucco weep screed along north patio wall is buried beneath decorative river rock and planter soil."*
- The SPCB Pest Inspector notes in Section 1: *"Drywood termite pellets observed in attic crawl space; complete structural tent fumigation recommended."*
- The Seller checks "No" to structural damage on the SPQ, writing: *"Routine preventative pest control service maintained with local provider."*

When evaluated separately, each statement seems routine. But when cross-referenced, they reveal a dangerous scenario: subterranean termites entered behind the buried stucco along the patio wall to hollow out bottom sill plates, while drywood termites established widespread colonies in the attic framing that localized spot treatments cannot cure.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Rancho Cucamonga

### 1. Are termites common in Rancho Cucamonga, California?
Yes. Rancho Cucamonga properties face intense pressure from both Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*) and Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*). Foothill wind corridors through the Cajon Pass, concrete slab plumbing penetrations, and buried stucco weep screeds create optimal conditions for termite colonies.

### 2. What are California sellers legally required to disclose regarding termites?
Under California Civil Code § 1102 (TDS) and the C.A.R. Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ), sellers are legally obligated to disclose all known active infestations, prior wood damage, historical chemical treatments, and past pest inspection reports. Intentionally concealing known defects exposes sellers to liability for fraud and actual damages.

### 3. What is the difference between Section 1 and Section 2 on a California pest report?
In a California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) inspection report, **Section 1** identifies active infestations, active dry rot, or visible structural wood damage requiring immediate repair. **Section 2** identifies conditions deemed conducive to future infestations (e.g., plumbing leaks, earth-to-wood contact, poor ventilation) that should be corrected preventatively.

### 4. Does homeowners insurance cover termite damage in California?
No. Standard California homeowners insurance policies exclude damage caused by termites, dry rot, and wood-destroying pests. Insurance carriers classify termite infestations as preventable maintenance issues, requiring homeowners to pay for all structural, fumigation, and carpentry repairs out of pocket.
