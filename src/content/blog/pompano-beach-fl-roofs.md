## Why Pompano Beach Roofs Fail: The High-Velocity Hurricane Zone and Marine Atmosphere

Pompano Beach is situated entirely within the Florida Building Code's High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ), an area subject to the strictest structural codes in the United States due to extreme hurricane vulnerability.

### High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) Forces and Dynamic Wind Uplift
Residential roofs in Pompano Beach are engineered to withstand Category 4 and 5 hurricane forces:
- **Extreme Aerodynamic Suction:** Hurricanes generating sustained winds over 140 mph produce immense negative pressure along roof ridges, hips, and eaves. While heavy concrete or clay barrel tiles are designed to resist direct wind, wind uplift vibrates tiles loose, breaking brittle mortar packs and tearing mechanical tile clips.
- **Projectile Tile Damage:** Loose tiles become airborne projectiles during major hurricanes, striking neighboring roof planes and shattering underlying membranes.
- **Horizontal Wind-Driven Torrential Rain:** Tropical cyclones routinely dump 12 to 20 inches of rain in 24 hours. High winds force rainwater sideways beneath tile interlocking channels. On roofs where the underlying waterproof felt has deteriorated, water immediately enters the building envelope.

### Airborne Saline Mist and Accelerated Galvanic Corrosion
Situated directly along the Atlantic Ocean and Intracoastal Waterway, Pompano Beach is blanketed by salt-laden coastal breezes:
- **Fastener Pitting and Failure:** Saline aerosols penetrate tile assemblies, corroding galvanized roofing nails, tile screws, and aluminum step flashings. As fasteners corrode, they expand, cracking surrounding timber decking and losing mechanical holding power.
- **Valley Pan and Flashing Perforation:** Corroded metal valley flashings develop microscopic pinholes along soldered seams. Water seeps into adjacent timber rafters and ceiling framing unnoticed until catastrophic wood decay develops.

### Intense Solar UV Radiation and Tile Underlayment Decomposition
South Florida experiences over 245 days of intense subtropical sunshine each year:
- **The Barrel Tile Paradox:** Homebuyers frequently assume that because concrete barrel tiles have a 40-to-50-year structural lifespan, the roof is virtually maintenance-free. However, the tiles are merely an aesthetic and physical shield; the true waterproof membrane is the asphalt underlayment installed underneath.
- **Thermal Baking and Felt Disintegration:** Direct summer sun heats dark barrel tiles to over 170°F. This extreme heat conducts downward into the underlying organic felt or modified bitumen underlayment. Over 15 to 20 years, the asphalt binders evaporate, leaving the underlayment brittle, cracked, and crumbly like dried paper.
- **Catastrophic Leaks During Tropical Squalls:** When severe tropical squalls strike, water naturally slips beneath the tiles. If the underlayment has deteriorated, water pours directly onto the plywood roof decking, soaking ceiling drywall while the exterior tiles appear intact.

## Understanding Florida Real Estate Disclosure Laws & The *Johnson v. Davis* Doctrine

Florida real estate law enforces strict legal standards governing seller disclosure, anchored by landmark Supreme Court precedent and statutory building regulations.

### The *Johnson v. Davis* Affirmative Duty of Disclosure
In the landmark decision ***Johnson v. Davis*, 480 So. 2d 625 (Fla. 1985)**, the Florida Supreme Court revolutionized residential real estate disclosure:
- **Affirmative Legal Duty:** The court established that where a seller of a home knows of facts materially affecting the value of the property which are not readily observable and are not known to the buyer, the seller is under an affirmative legal duty to disclose them.
- **"As-Is" Contracts Do Not Shield Fraud:** Under established Florida appellate law (e.g., ***Rayner v. Wise Realty Co.*, 504 So. 2d 1361 (Fla. 1st DCA 1987))**, standard "As-Is" contract clauses (such as the standard Florida Realtors/Florida Bar "AS IS" Residential Contract) do **not** protect a seller from liability for fraudulent concealment or failure to disclose known material roof defects.

### The Florida Realtors Property Disclosure Statement
While Florida does not have a mandatory state-drafted statutory disclosure form, standard practice utilizes the **Florida Realtors Seller's Property Disclosure - Residential (SPDR)**:
- **Section 5 (Roof):** The seller must state the approximate age of the roof covering and whether a full or partial replacement was performed during their ownership.
- **Leak History:** The seller must explicitly answer: *"Has the roof leaked during your ownership? If yes, what was done to address the leak?"*
- **Unpermitted Repairs:** The seller must disclose any structural roof repairs or reroofing completed without required municipal permits from the City of Pompano Beach.
- **Insurance Claims:** Sellers must declare whether any insurance claims were filed for roof damage and whether insurance proceeds were used to complete repairs.

### High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) Protocols & The Florida 25% Rule
The **City of Pompano Beach Building Inspections Division** enforces Chapter 15 of the Florida Building Code (HVHZ protocols):
- **Building Permits Required:** A residential building permit is legally mandatory for any reroofing project exceeding 100 square feet.
- **HVHZ Testing Protocols (TAS 105, TAS 106, TAS 123):** Roofing materials installed in Pompano Beach must possess valid Florida Product Approvals or Miami-Dade Notices of Acceptance (NOA), certifying resistance to 170+ mph winds and projectile impact.
- **The Florida 25% Rule (FBC Existing Building § 706.1.1):** Under Florida law, if more than 25% of the total roof surface of a building is repaired, replaced, or recovered within any 12-month period, the **entire roofing system must be brought into compliance with current HVHZ codes**—requiring a complete tear-off and replacement.
- **Mandatory Secondary Water Barrier:** HVHZ code requires a fully adhered self-adhered polymer-modified bitumen secondary water barrier directly over the structural plywood deck to protect the living areas if tiles dislodge.

## The Manual Review Problem: Navigating South Florida Due Diligence

In a standard Florida Realtors/Florida Bar (FR/BAR) "AS IS" contract, buyers typically negotiate an **Inspection Period spanning 10 to 15 calendar days**.

During this narrow window, prospective purchasers must cross-examine voluminous documentation:
- The completed Florida Realtors Property Disclosure Statement.
- City of Pompano Beach Building Department permit records to verify whether past reroofing projects complied with HVHZ protocols and passed final inspection.
- The certified Four-Point Inspection and Florida Windstorm Mitigation Inspection (Form OIR-B1-1802).
- Comprehensive C.L.U.E. insurance loss history reports detailing past hurricane and wind claims.
- 50-to-75-page general home inspection reports where inspectors disclaimer tile roofs with statements like: *"Tile roof viewed from ground level; walking barrel tiles is prohibited due to the risk of tile breakage."*

### Vague Phrasing Sellers Use to Disguise Roof Damage
Sellers and listing agents in Pompano Beach frequently employ ambiguous or evasive language on disclosure forms to avoid paying for an expensive roof replacement:
- *"Barrel tile roof inspected after recent hurricane; minor maintenance completed by local roofer."* (Conceals that an unpermitted repair patched cracked underlayment with silicone mastic).
- *"Cosmetic hairline cracks noted in several mortar cap joints; no active interior leaks."* (Masks fractured underlayment that will fail completely during subsequent tropical squalls).
- *"Roof original to home; in good working order."* (Conceals 22-year-old organic felt underlayment that is on the verge of complete water penetration).
- *"Insurance claim filed in 2023 for storm damage; claim resolved."* (Conceals that the seller pocketed the insurance payout and never replaced the roof).

Combing through dozens of disclosure pages while trying to decipher these deceptive patterns before your inspection period ends leaves buyers exposed to massive unexpected replacement costs.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in Pompano Beach, FL

### 1. Does the City of Pompano Beach require a building permit for roof replacement?
Yes. The City of Pompano Beach Building Inspections Division requires a building permit for all reroofing projects exceeding 100 square feet. Permitted installations must comply with the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) requirements of the Florida Building Code. City building inspectors conduct multiple in-progress inspections—including deck re-nailing, secondary water barrier adhesion, and tile attachment—before granting final approval.

### 2. Why do barrel tile roofs in Pompano Beach leak when the tiles look undamaged?
Concrete and clay barrel tiles are designed to deflect direct water and resist physical weathering, but they do not form a hermetic waterproof seal. The true waterproof barrier is the asphalt-saturated underlayment installed underneath. In Pompano Beach's hot climate, intense solar heat conducts through tiles, baking the underlying felt until it becomes brittle and cracks. Once the underlayment fails, water penetrates the roof deck even if every tile is intact.

### 3. What is a "tile lift and relay," and when is it necessary?
A "tile lift and relay" is a specialized restoration process where existing, undamaged concrete or clay barrel tiles are carefully removed and set aside. Roofers tear off the deteriorated underlayment, repair damaged plywood decking, install modern high-temperature self-adhering modified bitumen underlayment, and then reinstall the original tiles. This restores full waterproof integrity while preserving the original architectural aesthetic.

### 4. What legal remedies exist under *Johnson v. Davis* if a seller concealed roof leaks?
Under the Florida Supreme Court doctrine *Johnson v. Davis*, sellers have an affirmative duty to disclose known latent defects affecting property value that are not readily observable by the buyer. An "as-is" clause does not shield a seller from fraudulent concealment. If a buyer discovers unpermitted roof repairs or concealed leaks after closing, they can sue the seller for actual repair damages, loss in property value, and fraud.
