## The Hidden Threat: Termites in Pomona and the Eastern LA County Foothills

Pomona's location at the intersection of the San Gabriel and Pomona Valleys, combined with its historical agricultural soil conditions, creates distinct structural pest vulnerabilities.

### Why Pomona Housing Stock and Valley Climate Elevate Pest Risk

Several construction practices, soil characteristics, and climatic elements intensify termite hazards in Pomona homes:

- **Decaying Citrus Root Systems and Subterranean Colony Networks:** Before mid-20th-century suburban development, Pomona was blanketed in extensive orange and citrus orchards. Decomposing subterranean taproots throughout neighborhoods like Westmont and Phillips Ranch provide persistent underground food sources, sustaining large colonies of Western Subterranean Termites.
- **Historic Crawlspace Framing in Lincoln Park & Wilton Heights:** Early 20th-century Craftsman and Spanish Revival homes feature raised perimeter foundations with unsealed dirt crawlspaces, low ground clearance, and direct earth-to-wood contact at the mudsills. Damp winter conditions encourage subterranean termites to tunnel up interior concrete piers directly into antique Douglas fir floor joists.
- **Buried Weep Screeds on Phillips Ranch Hillside Slabs:** In 1980s and 1990s master-planned communities like Phillips Ranch, homes feature monolithic slab foundations with exterior stucco. Hillside soil movement, decorative planter beds, and landscape mulch frequently bury the metal weep screed, allowing subterranean termites to enter behind the stucco directly into load-bearing wall studs.
- **Architectural Vulnerabilities Across Pomona Neighborhoods:**
  - *Lincoln Park & Wilton Heights:* 1900s–1930s Craftsman bungalows and Spanish Colonial Revivals with exposed exterior wood rafter tails, wood siding, and crawlspaces vulnerable to both subterranean and drywood termites.
  - *Ganesha Hills:* Custom mid-century ranch homes built on sloping terrain where foundation retaining walls experience high moisture pressure.
  - *Phillips Ranch:* 1980s–1990s multi-story stucco homes with complex tile rooflines where drywood swarmers colonize unsealed attic vents.

### Pest Biology: Subterranean vs. Drywood Termites in Eastern LA County

Pomona homebuyers must understand the two major wood-destroying insect species active in the area:

- **Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** The most widespread subterranean termite in Southern California. Colonies live underground and build earthen mud shelter tubes across foundation walls, concrete slabs, and plumbing lines to reach structural framing, hollowing out the soft interior grain of mudsills, floor joists, and wall studs.
- **Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*):** Non-soil-dwelling insects that live entirely within structural timber. They enter through attic vents, fascia boards, and window trim during late summer swarms, carving galleries across roof trusses without needing ground moisture and expelling distinct six-sided fecal pellets (frass).

## California Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Civil Code § 1102 and SPCB Branch 3 Reports

California enforces rigorous statutory disclosure laws designed to protect homebuyers from undisclosed property defects.

### The Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and SPQ

Under **California Civil Code § 1102 et seq.**, sellers must provide two essential disclosure forms: the statutory **Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)** and the California Association of Realtors (C.A.R.) **Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)**:

- **TDS Section II.C:** Asks sellers whether they are aware of any *"settling, slippage, sliding, or other soil problems"* or *"Flooding, drainage or grading problems,"* both of which frequently correlate with subterranean termite entry points.
- **SPQ Section V (Pests, Infestations, and Soil):** Directly inquires whether the seller has knowledge of:
  - Known active or past pest infestations, including termites, dry rot, or wood-destroying beetles.
  - Previous pest control treatments, chemical applications, or service contracts.
  - Repairs, alterations, or structural replacements resulting from pest damage.

Under California law, a seller who intentionally fails to disclose known termite swarms, past localized treatments, or pest inspection reports is liable for fraudulent concealment or negligent misrepresentation.

### Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) Branch 3 Reports: Section 1 vs. Section 2

In California, official Wood Destroying Pest and Organisms (WDO) inspection reports are conducted by licensed Branch 3 pest control companies regulated by the **Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB)**:

- **Section 1 Findings (Active Infestations / Direct Damage):** Documents visible active colonies of subterranean or drywood termites, wood-decay fungus, and compromised structural timbers requiring immediate eradication and physical repair.
- **Section 2 Conditions (Conducive Conditions):** Documents conditions that do not show active infestation but are likely to lead to one, such as buried stucco weep screeds, earth-to-wood contact, plumbing leaks, or cracked perimeter seals.

### What Pomona Sellers Reveal vs. Strategically Conceal

In competitive Southern California transactions, sellers frequently employ vague phrasing on disclosure forms:
- *"Localized spot treatment completed on garage eaves in 2022."* (Conceals that spot treatment was chosen to avoid the cost of whole-house tent fumigation).
- *"Fascia board painted and touched up during exterior refresh."* (Masks the fact that termite-hollowed rafter tails were patched with wood filler rather than replaced).
- *"Bait stations maintained by previous pest company."* (Downplays that baiting was initiated following an active subterranean swarm into the living room wall).

## The Manual Review Problem: Overlooking Crucial Clues in 200-Page Closing Packets

During California's standard 17-day buyer investigation period under the C.A.R. Residential Purchase Agreement (RPA), buyers must review an enormous volume of technical documentation:
- C.A.R. Residential Purchase Agreement, counteroffers, and contingency addenda.
- Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ) and statutory Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS).
- 50-to-80-page Comprehensive General Home Inspection Report.
- SPCB Branch 3 Structural Pest Control Report with Section 1 and Section 2 repair estimates.
- Los Angeles County Natural Hazard Disclosures (NHD), preliminary title reports, and municipal permit histories.

### The Danger of Disconnected Clues

Critical structural hazards rarely appear as an explicit, single warning. Instead, vital indicators are fragmented across separate disclosures:
- The Home Inspector writes on page 41: *"Planter bed soil is piled above the stucco weep screed on the west wall."*
- The SPCB Pest Inspector records under Inaccessible Areas: *"Drywall interior and exterior stucco prevented visual inspection of internal wall studs."*
- The Seller checks "Yes" to prior treatments on the SPQ, writing: *"Preventative exterior spot treatment performed in 2023."*

Individually, these notes seem minor. But when cross-referenced, the danger becomes clear: buried weep screeds allowed subterranean termites to bypass exterior inspection, crawl behind the stucco, and hollow out load-bearing wall studs along the west elevation.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Pomona

### 1. Are termites common in Pomona, California?
Yes. Both Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*) and Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*) are widespread throughout Pomona and eastern Los Angeles County. Hot inland summers, decomposing citrus orchard root networks, and historic housing stock create ideal conditions for wood-destroying insect infestations.

### 2. What are California sellers required to disclose regarding termites?
Under California Civil Code § 1102 and the standard Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ), sellers must disclose all known active or past termite infestations, pest damage, previous treatments, and copies of any pest control inspection reports or warranties in their possession.

### 3. What is the difference between Section 1 and Section 2 on a California Pest Report?
On an SPCB Branch 3 Pest Report, **Section 1** items represent active wood-destroying infestations or direct physical damage that requires immediate treatment or structural repair. **Section 2** items represent conducive conditions (such as earth-to-wood contact, buried stucco weep screeds, or plumbing leaks) that could lead to future infestation if left uncorrected.

### 4. Does homeowners insurance cover termite damage repairs in California?
No. Standard California homeowners insurance policies strictly exclude damage caused by termites, dry rot, and other wood-destroying organisms. Insurance carriers classify pest damage as a preventable property maintenance issue, leaving property owners responsible for all structural and cosmetic repair costs.
