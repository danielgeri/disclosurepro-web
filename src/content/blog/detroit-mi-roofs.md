# The Ultimate Guide to Spotting Roof Damage in Detroit Real Estate Disclosures

Detroit is experiencing one of the most remarkable urban and architectural revivals in the nation. Across Detroit’s older housing stock, decades of deferred maintenance and unpermitted repairs have left many roofing assemblies severely compromised. Overlooking vague phrasing or deliberate omissions in a Michigan Seller’s Disclosure Statement can leave a buyer facing catastrophic structural restoration costs shortly after closing.

## The Anatomy of Detroit Roof Damage: Freeze-Thaw, Ice Dams, and Historic Decking

Navigating Wayne County’s historic housing stock requires a thorough technical understanding of how Michigan’s climate degrades residential roofing assemblies.

### The Great Lakes Ice Dam Menace
During long Detroit winters, snow accumulates on steep roof pitches. When warm indoor air escapes into under-insulated attics, it melts the underside of the snowpack:
- **Refreezing at Cold Eaves:** Meltwater cascades down the slope until it reaches the unheated roof eaves and overhanging gutters. In sub-freezing temperatures, this runoff instantly refreezes, forming a solid, growing barrier of ice—an **ice dam**.
- **Capillary Water Infiltration:** As snow continues to melt higher up the roof plane, water pools behind the ice barrier. Because traditional asphalt shingles and slates are designed solely to shed water downward, the standing water forces its way upward beneath shingle laps, penetrates basic felt underlayment, and enters the building envelope.
- **Structural Rafter and Cornice Rot:** Chronic winter ice damming rots structural rafter tails, destroys decorative exterior wooden cornices, and saturates attic insulation, triggering toxic mold growth behind historic plaster walls.

### The Historic Housing Stock: 100-Year-Old Plank Decking and Slate Transitions
A substantial portion of Detroit’s historic neighborhoods features homes built between 1910 and 1935:
- **Tongue-and-Groove Plank Decking:** Historic Detroit homes were framed with true 1-inch dimensional pine or fir tongue-and-groove planks rather than modern plywood or OSB. Over a century of seasonal expansion and drying, these boards shrink, cup, and develop wide gaps. When modern roofing nails hit these gaps, shingles fail to hold, blowing off in heavy spring windstorms.
- **Historic Slate and Copper Flashing Failures:** In neighborhoods like Palmer Woods and Boston-Edison, original roofs were constructed with heavy quarried slate tiles and soldered copper box gutters. After 90 to 100 years, copper valleys develop hairline fissures from thermal fatigue, and steel fasteners suffer "nail sickness," allowing heavy slates to slide down roof slopes.
- **Unpermitted Asphalt Conversions:** Unqualified contractors frequently attempt to replace historic slate with cheap asphalt shingles without installing continuous solid plywood sheathing or properly tying into historic masonry chimneys, leaving massive hidden leak paths.

### The Multi-Layer Overlay Trap
Decades of economic fluctuation in Detroit led many property owners and flippers to cut costs by nailing new shingle layers directly over old, failing roofs:
- **Excessive Structural Dead Load:** Asphalt shingles weigh between 200 and 400 pounds per square (100 square feet). Having three or four layers of shingles on a steep roof adds thousands of pounds of dead load onto 100-year-old timber rafters. Under heavy winter snow loads, this extra weight causes roof ridges to sag and rafters to crack.
- **Trapped Moisture and Rot:** Multiple shingle layers trap condensed moisture against the wood decking, accelerating fungal rot while masking the deterioration from visual ground inspections.

## Navigating Michigan Real Estate Disclosure Laws in Detroit

In Michigan, residential property sales are governed by the statutory framework of the Michigan Seller Disclosure Act, designed to mandate transparency regarding structural defects.

### The Michigan Seller Disclosure Act (MCL 565.951 et seq.)
Under MCL 565.957, sellers of 1-to-4 family residential properties must complete the standardized **Seller's Disclosure Statement (SDS)**.

The Michigan disclosure statement explicitly questions the seller regarding the roof:
- **Section 1 (Property Conditions):**
  - *"Roof: Approximate age, if known:"*
  - *"Are you aware of any current or previous leaks or other material problems with the roof?"*
  - *"Have there been any repairs or replacements made to the roof during your ownership?"*
- **Structural Integrity:** Inquires about known settling, rafter cracking, or water intrusion in attics and ceilings.

### Good Faith and the Limits of "As-Is" Clauses
Under MCL 565.960, every disclosure must be made in **good faith**. Michigan courts have repeatedly affirmed that sellers cannot hide behind boilerplate "as-is" contract clauses to shield themselves from intentional fraud or active concealment.

In landmark Michigan cases (such as *M&D, Inc. v. McConkey*), courts held that while sellers are not generally liable for innocent oversights if the buyer could discover them via inspection, **active fraudulent concealment**—such as painting over active attic water stains, removing water-damaged plaster immediately before listing, or lying about known roof leaks on the SDS—creates actionable liability for fraudulent misrepresentation.

### Evasive Phrasing Used by Detroit Sellers
Despite statutory mandates, sellers and flippers in Detroit frequently deploy evasive language to deflect liability:
- *"New roof installed in 2023"* (omitting the fact that the new shingles were overlaid on top of three failing historical layers without City of Detroit building permits).
- *"Roof inspected annually; gutters cleaned"* (disguising persistent winter ice dams and rotted eaves).
- *"Minor ceiling discoloration in upstairs bedroom from prior owner; no active leak"* (cosmetically primed with stain-blocking paint without addressing rotted valley flashing).
- *"Seller is an investor / LLC and has never lived in the property"* (used as a catch-all justification to check "Unknown" across all critical structural sections).

## The Manual Review Problem: Drowning in Detroit Due Diligence

In standard Detroit real estate contracts, buyers typically negotiate an inspection contingency window of **7 to 10 calendar days**. 

During this high-pressure period, prospective buyers must digest an overwhelming volume of complex records:
1. The 4-page Michigan Seller’s Disclosure Statement.
2. The City of Detroit Buildings, Safety Engineering, and Environmental Department (BSEED) permit history.
3. Historic District Commission (HDC) guidelines and certificates (for homes in Boston-Edison, Indian Village, or Corktown).
4. The comprehensive physical home inspection report (often 50 to 75 pages).
5. Lead, asbestos, and sewer lateral inspection reports.

Critical roofing defects are frequently buried in obscure inspection comments. For instance, a home inspector might include a single line on page 39: *"Observed multiple layers of composition shingles and moderate rafter deflection in southwest attic knee-wall."* To an untrained buyer, "moderate deflection" sounds benign. In reality, in Detroit’s heavy snow zone, a deflected rafter under multiple shingle layers indicates that the roof framing is on the verge of structural failure under the next winter blizzard.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in Detroit

### 1. How many roofing layers are legally permitted on a home in Detroit?
Under the Michigan Residential Code (MRC Section R908.3), a roof can have a maximum of **two layers of roof coverings**. If a Detroit home already has two layers, installing a third layer is a direct building code violation. Any subsequent roof work requires a complete tear-off down to the bare wooden decking. Multiple layers trap heat and moisture, add dangerous dead weight, and can invalidate shingle manufacturer warranties.

### 2. What special rules apply to roofs in Detroit Historic Districts?
If your property is situated within a designated City of Detroit Historic District (such as Boston-Edison, Indian Village, Palmer Woods, or Corktown), exterior roof alterations are strictly governed by the **Detroit Historic District Commission (HDC)**. Replacing historic slate or tile with modern asphalt shingles, altering copper trim, or removing built-in box gutters requires a formal **Certificate of Appropriateness (COA)**. Unpermitted work can result in stop-work orders, steep fines, and court orders mandating removal at the owner’s expense.

### 3. What is an Ice and Water Shield, and is it required in Detroit?
Yes. Under the Michigan Residential Code, an ice barrier membrane (Ice and Water Shield) is mandatory on all heated residential structures. The self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen membrane must be applied directly to the roof deck, extending from the eave edge to a point at least 24 inches inside the building’s exterior wall line. This barrier prevents water backed up behind winter ice dams from leaking into living areas.

### 4. What is the typical lifespan of an architectural asphalt shingle roof in Detroit?
In Southeast Michigan’s volatile climate—with extreme winter freezes, heavy snowloads, ice damming, and humid summer heatwaves—standard architectural shingles typically last **18 to 22 years**. Standard 3-tab shingles frequently fail in as few as 12 to 15 years, particularly when installed over poorly ventilated attics or historical plank decking.
