## The Technical Anatomy of a Roof in Illinois: Critical Components & Common Mistakes

To understand why residential roofs fail prematurely in the Midwest, homebuyers must evaluate the complete **anatomy of a roof in Illinois** and recognize where contractors cut corners. An asphalt or modified bitumen roof is not merely a waterproof outer skin; it is a complex, multi-layered envelope designed to shed precipitation while regulating attic thermodynamics.

```
       [ Ridge Vent / Exhaust ]
                  /\
                 /  \
 [Shingles]     /    \     [Underlayment / Felt]
               /      \
 [Decking]    /        \    [Ice & Water Shield (Min 24" inside wall)]
             /          \
            /            \
           / [Soffit Vent] \  [Drip Edge & Gutter]
```

### 1. Structural Roof Decking (Sheathing)
The structural foundation of any roof assembly is the decking. In modern construction, builders use 1/2-inch or 5/8-inch CDX plywood or Oriented Strand Board (OSB). In pre-1950 Chicago brick bungalows, two-flats, and Victorians, the decking typically consists of 1x6 or 1x8 tongue-and-groove pine or fir planks:
- **Common Roofing Mistake:** When re-roofing older plank decks, contractors often fail to replace rotted, delaminated, or widely spaced boards (exceeding 1/4-inch gaps). Shingle nails driven into gaps or decayed wood have zero holding power, allowing 60 mph winter winds off Lake Michigan to tear off entire shingle fields.

### 2. Drip Edge and Gutter Aprons
A corrosion-resistant metal drip edge must be installed along eaves and rakes to direct runoff cleanly away from fascia boards and into gutters:
- **Common Roofing Mistake:** In Illinois, amateur installers frequently skip drip edges along rakes or install the drip edge *over* the underlayment at the eaves rather than directly against the wood decking beneath the ice barrier. This directs capillary meltwater directly behind the gutter, rotting the wooden fascia and roof overhang framing.

### 3. Self-Adhering Ice and Water Shield (Underlayment)
In cold climates subject to freeze-thaw cycles, building codes mandate self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen underlayment along eaves:
- **Code Standard (IRC R905.1.2 / Chicago Energy & Building Code):** Ice barrier membranes must extend from the lowest edge of the roof surface to a point at least **24 inches inside the interior exterior wall line** of the heated building envelope.
- **Common Roofing Mistake:** Cut-rate roofers frequently install only a single 36-inch strip along the eave. On wide overhangs or low-slope sections (less than 4:12 pitch), a single roll fails to reach the required 24 inches past the interior wall plane, leaving the critical thermal junction completely vulnerable to ice dam penetration.

### 4. Flashing Assemblies: Step, Counter, and Valley
Flashing prevents water intrusion where roof planes intersect vertical walls, dormers, and masonry chimneys:
- **Step Flashing vs. Continuous L-Flashing:** Step flashing involves individual L-shaped metal pieces woven into every shingle course along sidewalls. A widespread installation error is using single continuous pieces of flashing, which buckle during thermal expansion and channel water into wall cavities.
- **Chimney Crickets (Saddles):** Under **IRC R905.2.8.3**, chimneys with a width greater than 30 inches parallel to the roof ridge require a sheet metal or framed **cricket (saddle)** on the upslope side to divert runoff. Omitting crickets on Chicago brick chimneys creates a debris-collecting shelf that guarantees masonry saturation and attic leaks.

### 5. Attic Ventilation Equilibrium: Soffits vs. Ridge Vents
Proper roof anatomy requires continuous airflow from intake vents (soffits) to exhaust vents (ridge or box vents) at a balanced 1:300 ventilation ratio:
- **Common Roofing Mistake:** Insulation contractors blow loose-fill cellulose or fiberglass directly into eaves, blocking soffit baffles. Trapped humid air from interior living spaces accumulates in the attic during winter, condensing on cold roof nails (often mistaken for roof leaks) and rotting the sheathing from the inside out.

## Chicago Freeze-Thaw Ice Dams: The Silent Structural Destroyer

The Chicago metropolitan area endures prolonged periods of sub-zero temperatures followed by rapid warming spikes. This atmospheric volatility produces destructive **ice dams**:

1. **The Thermal Loop:** Warm indoor air escapes into unconditioned attics through unsealed can lights, attic bypasses, and uninsulated knee walls. This elevates attic temperatures above 32°F, melting snow on the upper roof slopes.
2. **Refreezing at Cold Overhangs:** The meltwater runs down to unheated eaves and gutters (which remain below 32°F), instantly freezing into an expanding ice ridge.
3. **Hydrostatic Lift:** Trapped meltwater accumulates behind the dam. Because asphalt shingles are designed solely to shed water through gravity, standing water backs up underneath shingles by capillary action.
4. **Structural Degradation:** Once water penetrates the decking, it saturates attic insulation (slashing R-value), rots 2x4 framing, creates black mold blooms, and streams down through interior drywall ceilings.

In older Chicago two-flats and multi-family buildings with flat modified bitumen or tar-and-gravel roofs, failing internal roof drains and clogged scuppers cause standing ponding water to freeze into ice lenses, tearing membrane laps and triggering catastrophic interior ceiling collapses.

## Building Codes & The Two-Layer Tear-Off Rule (IRC R908.3.1)

One of the most dangerous and expensive roofing traps in Illinois real estate transactions is the unpermitted **second layer (layover) roof**.

### The Statutory Code Limit
Under the **International Residential Code (IRC) Section R908.3.1** (adopted across Illinois municipalities) and the **Chicago Building Code (Title 14R)**:
- A new roof covering shall **not** be installed over an existing roof covering where the existing roof already has **two or more applications** of any type of roof covering.
- A roof recover is prohibited if the existing roof covering is water-soaked or deteriorated to the point where it cannot serve as an adequate base for additional roofing materials.
- A recover is prohibited over wood shake, slate, clay, or cement tile.

### Why Layovers Threaten Buyers
Unscrupulous property flippers and cash-strapped sellers frequently nail a second layer of architectural shingles directly over curling 3-tab shingles rather than paying for a complete tear-off:
- **Excess Structural Dead Load:** Two layers of asphalt shingles add up to 400–500 pounds per 100 square feet. Under a heavy 20 lb/cu.ft. Chicago snowload, this excessive dead weight causes roof rafter deflection, sagging ridges, and ceiling drywall cracking.
- **Concealed Decking Decay:** A layover buries rotten wood decking and hidden moisture. Inspectors cannot evaluate the condition of the substrate beneath two layers.
- **Accelerated Thermal Degradation:** The bottom shingle layer traps solar heat, baking the top shingles and reducing a 30-year architectural shingle's lifespan to just 12 to 15 years.
- **Double Tear-Off Costs:** When the roof inevitably fails, the buyer must pay for a **double tear-off and disposal fee**, dramatically driving up replacement costs.

## Illinois Seller Disclosure Law: 765 ILCS 77 Requirements & Remedies

Unlike caveat emptor states where sellers can legally remain silent, Illinois provides statutory protections to residential buyers under the **Residential Real Property Disclosure Act (765 ILCS 77)**.

### Mandatory Disclosure Questions
Before an Illinois purchase contract is executed, sellers of residential properties (1 to 4 units) must provide the statutory disclosure report. Key questions regarding roofing include:
- **Line 2 / Question 2:** *"I am aware of material defects in the roof, ceilings, or chimney."*
- **Line 1 / Question 1:** *"I am aware of flooding or recurring leakage problems in the crawl space or basement."* (Often linked to failed roof downspouts or gutter overflows).

Under 765 ILCS 77/35, a **material defect** is defined as a condition that would have a substantial adverse effect on the value of the property or that would significantly impair the health or safety of future occupants.

### Good Faith vs. Intentional Concealment
While the statute does not obligate an Illinois seller to hire an independent roofer or climb onto the roof to discover unknown defects, sellers must complete the form in **good faith based on actual knowledge**:
- If a seller experienced roof leaks, placed buckets in the attic during spring storms, or hired a handyman to apply silicone sealant over a flashing seam, checking "NO" on the disclosure form constitutes fraudulent misrepresentation.
- If a seller repaints ceiling water stains in living areas immediately before listing without repairing the underlying roof intrusion, this constitutes active concealment.

### Statutory Remedies Under 765 ILCS 77/55
Under **765 ILCS 77/55**, if a seller knowingly delivers a false or inaccurate disclosure statement:
- The seller is liable for **actual damages** suffered by the buyer (the full cost of repairing or replacing the defective roof assembly).
- The court may award **court costs and reasonable attorney fees** to the prevailing buyer.
- If the disclosure report is delivered to the buyer *after* the purchase contract is executed, the buyer holds an absolute right to **terminate the contract within 5 business days** of receipt and recover all earnest money.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Illinois Roof Inspections

### What does 765 ILCS 77 require Illinois sellers to disclose about roof leaks?
Under 765 ILCS 77 Section 35, residential sellers in Illinois must disclose any known material defects in the roof, ceilings, or chimneys. A material defect is defined as any issue that substantially impacts property value or occupant safety. While sellers are not required to conduct independent roof inspections, they cannot legally conceal past or present leaks, recurring water stains, or unpermitted roofing work known to them.

### Can you put a second layer of shingles on a roof in Illinois?
Building codes in Illinois (IRC Section R908.3.1 and local municipal codes) permit a maximum of two layers of roof coverings. However, installing a second layer is strictly prohibited if the existing roof is water-soaked, if the decking is deteriorated, or if the existing covering is wood shake, tile, or slate. If a home already has two layers, the entire assembly must be torn off down to bare decking before installing a new roof.

### Why do ice dams cause severe roof damage in Chicago homes?
Ice dams form in Chicago homes when heat escapes from living areas into poorly insulated or improperly ventilated attics, melting snow on the upper roof. The meltwater runs down to cold, unheated eaves and freezes into an ice ridge. As water pools behind this ice dam, hydrostatic pressure forces moisture upward beneath shingles and underlayment, rotting wooden roof decking, soaking ceiling insulation, and damaging interior plaster and drywall.

### How do you negotiate a roof replacement credit after an Illinois home inspection?
During the contractual 5-business-day inspection and attorney review period, obtain written estimates from licensed Illinois roofing contractors documenting required code upgrades and tear-off costs. Submit a formal repair credit request through your real estate attorney requesting a closing cost concession or purchase price reduction rather than permitting the seller to perform cut-rate repairs.
