Opening a 90-page home inspection report and seeing **47 flagged items** can feel overwhelming—especially when your inspection or due-diligence contingency expires in 72 hours. Ask for too little and you inherit **$20,000+ in surprise repairs** the month you move in; send the seller a messy laundry list of 47 cosmetic touch-ups (loose cabinet knobs, missing outlet faceplates, torn window screens) and you risk offending the seller into rejecting your request outright.

Knowing **what** to ask for, **how** to document it with exact page citations, and **how** to structure the concession so your mortgage underwriter approves it seamlessly is the difference between walking away with $500 and negotiating a **$12,000 to $25,000+ seller credit**.

---

## Seller Closing Credit vs. Seller Pre-Close Repairs vs. Price Reduction

When major defects surface in your inspection or disclosure packet, you have three ways to structure compensation. In nearly all financed transactions, one option beats the other two handily:

### 1. Seller Credit Toward Buyer's Closing Costs (Usually Best)
Instead of asking the seller to hire contractors before closing, the seller credits a fixed dollar sum (for example, **$15,000**) directly against your recurring and non-recurring closing costs—including loan origination points, title insurance, escrow fees, prepaid property taxes, hazard insurance, and **mortgage rate buydowns**.
- **Why buyers win:** You keep $15,000 of your own cash in your bank account at closing to hire **your own vetted, licensed contractors** who put the warranty in your name—rather than letting an exiting seller hire their cousin's handyman for the cheapest cosmetic patch job imaginable.
- **Why sellers accept:** Moving out is stressful; most sellers gladly trade a clean closing credit over coordinating roofers, plumbers, and city inspectors while packing boxes.

### 2. Purchase Price Reduction
Lowering the contract price from $650,000 to $635,000 sounds identical to a $15,000 closing credit—until you check the math at the closing table.
- If you are putting **20% down**, a $15,000 price cut only reduces your cash needed at closing by **$3,000** (and trims your monthly mortgage payment by roughly $75/month), leaving you **$12,000 short on liquid cash** to actually pay the roofer or electrician the week after closing!
- **When to use a price reduction instead:** Only when your total seller credits exceed your **lender's maximum Interested Party Contribution (IPC) cap** or exceed your total allowable closing costs + rate buydown points!

### 3. Seller-Performed Repairs Prior to Close
Reserve seller-completed repairs strictly for **lender-mandated or insurer-mandated blockers** that *must* be cured before your loan can fund—such as VA loan [Section 1 termite and dry rot clearances in California](/blog/section-1-vs-section-2-termite-report-california/), missing water-heater seismic strapping/CO detectors required by state law, active plumbing gushers, or open permits the current owner already started. Whenever a seller performs work, require **licensed, insured contractors, finaled permits if applicable, and paid transferable warranty receipts** delivered at least 5 days before closing.

## Crucial Mortgage Underwriting Rule: Never Sabotage Your Own Loan!

Here is a costly rookie mistake: a buyer negotiates a $14,000 credit and writes on the Purchase Agreement Addendum:

> ❌ **BAD ADDENDUM WORDING:** *"Seller to credit Buyer $14,000 at closing for active roof leaks, Zinsco fire-hazard electrical panel replacement, and foundation crack repairs."*

Every contract addendum must be sent to your **mortgage lender's underwriter**. The moment an underwriter sees the words *"active roof leaks," "fire hazard,"* or *"foundation crack"* on a signed contract addendum, Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac/FHA guidelines frequently force the underwriter to **freeze loan approval**, demand the full home inspection report, and require **all physical repairs to be completed and re-inspected by the appraiser PRIOR to closing!**

Instead, separate your **negotiation justification letter** (or inspection request form that stays between buyer, seller, and agents depending on state forms) from the **financial contract addendum** sent to escrow and underwriting:

> ✅ **CLEAN FINANCIAL ADDENDUM WORDING:** *"Seller agrees to credit Buyer the sum of $14,000 toward Buyer's recurring and non-recurring closing costs, prepaids, and/or discount points at close of escrow."*

*(Always confirm exact form mechanics with your licensed real estate agent, escrow officer, and loan officer—for instance, California's C.A.R. Request for Repair `RR` form vs. Addendum `ADM`, or [Texas's TREC Amendment form during the Option Period](/blog/texas-trec-sellers-disclosure-guide/).)*

## Check Your Lender's Maximum Seller Credit Caps First

Before asking for a large credit, ask your loan officer two numbers: **(1) your total allowable closing costs + discount points**, and **(2) your loan program's Interested Party Contribution (IPC) cap**:

- **Conventional Primary / Second Home (<10% Down Payment):** Max **3%** of purchase price
- **Conventional Primary / Second Home (10% – 24.99% Down):** Max **6%** of purchase price
- **Conventional Primary / Second Home (25%+ Down):** Max **9%** of purchase price
- **Conventional Investment Property (Any Down Payment):** Max **2%** of purchase price
- **FHA Loans:** Max **6%** of purchase price
- **VA Loans:** Up to **4%** in specific VA seller concessions (plus standard reasonable closing costs)

**Pro Tip:** What if your closing costs are $11,000, but you uncover a $20,000 roof defect? Any unused closing credit above actual closing costs vanishes—you cannot get "cash back" at closing. Instead, either apply the extra $9,000 to **permanently buy down your mortgage interest rate** (which counts as a valid closing cost!) OR split the $20,000 as an **$11,000 closing credit + $9,000 purchase price reduction**!

## The 4-Step Formula for Getting Sellers to Say "Yes"

Listing agents push back hardest against vague, emotional numbers (*"We want $20,000 off because the house needs work"*). Sellers cave quickly when presented with **undisclosed, safety-critical, page-cited evidence**—because once you hand a seller written documentation of a material defect, **they are legally required to disclose that exact report to every future buyer if they let your deal fall through!**

1. **Focus Only on the "Big 5" Material Categories:** Limit your negotiation rationale to **Roofing/Water Intrusion**, **Electrical Safety** (such as [Zinsco or Federal Pacific breaker panels](/blog/zinsco-federal-pacific-electrical-panel-disclosure/)), **Plumbing/Sewer Main**, **HVAC/Mechanical**, and **Structural/Pest/HOA Assessments** (see [spotting special assessments in HOA reserve studies](/blog/hoa-reserve-study-special-assessment-red-flags/)). Ignore $30 GFCI outlets and dripping faucets.
2. **Distinguish "Undisclosed Defects" From Visible Age:** Sellers argue *"You saw the 1970s kitchen when you toured."* Counter by highlighting defects **contradicted or omitted** in the seller's disclosures—for example, *"TDS Section II checked 'No' for roof leaks, whereas Inspection Report p. 38 documents active moisture meter readings in primary attic sheathing."*
3. **Attach Itemized Cost Ranges or Contractor Bids:** Show transparent math rather than a round arbitrary number (e.g., $4,800 electrical panel upgrade + $9,400 flat-roof membrane replacement = $14,200 credit request).
4. **Keep Your Tone Collaborative:** Frame the credit as the fastest way to keep closing on schedule without reopening the property to weeks of contractor walkthroughs.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Repair Credit Negotiations

### Can I still ask for a repair credit if the listing says "Sold As-Is"?
Yes, as long as you still have an active **inspection, due diligence, or option contingency**. "As-Is" simply signals the seller's starting posture—if walking away forces the seller to relist the property *with* your newly discovered structural or sewer defect attached to their updated disclosures, rational sellers negotiate every day.

### What if my inspection period expires tomorrow and contractors haven't sent quotes yet?
Never let an inspection contingency or Texas Option Period expire passively while waiting on verbal promises! Either submit a written **Contingency Extension Addendum** signed by the seller *before* the deadline, or submit your formal repair credit request using conservative documented repair ranges while keeping your termination right protected.
