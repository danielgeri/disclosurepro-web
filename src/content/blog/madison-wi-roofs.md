## Why Madison Roofs Fail: Isthmus Blizzards, Destructive Ice Dams, and Glacial Freeze-Thaw

Evaluating residential roofing systems in Madison requires understanding how extreme Midwest winter weather intersects with historic timber-framed construction.

### The Anatomy of Destructive Madison Ice Dams
Ice dams represent the single most common and destructive roofing vulnerability in Dane County:
- **Attic Thermal Bypasses in Historic Homes:** Madison’s vintage Craftsman bungalows and Victorian homes frequently feature unsealed attic bypasses—open plumbing chases, uninsulated recessed lighting, unsealed chimney flues, and inadequate attic floor insulation. Heat from the living space continuously leaks into the attic, warming the underside of the upper roof deck.
- **The Snow Melt and Refreeze Cycle:** Snow resting on the upper roof melts, flowing down the slope as liquid water. When this runoff reaches the cold eaves, overhangs, and unheated gutters (which hover at ambient freezing temperatures), the meltwater refreezes into a thick ice ridge.
- **Hydrostatic Shingle Back-Up:** As melting continues, water pools behind the ice dam. Because standard asphalt shingles and cedar shakes are designed only to shed water running downward by gravity, the backed-up standing water forces its way underneath shingle tabs, bypassing underlayment, rotting wooden roof decking, and soaking attic fiberglass insulation.

### Heavy Snowpack and Rafter Deflection
Madison winters routinely deliver dense, moisture-laden snowfall:
- **Excessive Snow Loads:** Fresh powdery snow weighs roughly 5 to 7 pounds per cubic foot, but compacted, rain-soaked snowpack and ice can easily weigh 20 to 30 pounds per cubic foot. On multi-pitch roofs with complex valleys—common in historic Vilas and Shorewood Hills estates—drifting snow can exert point loads exceeding 50 pounds per square foot.
- **Structural Rafter Sag:** Many historic Madison homes were framed with true-dimensional 2x4 or 2x6 rafters on 24-inch centers spanning long distances without collar ties or knee walls. Decades of heavy snow loads cause progressive rafter creep, creating noticeable dips and sags across roof planes that pool water and strain structural joints.

### Chimney Spalling and Valley Flashing Degradation
Madison’s historic neighborhoods feature prominent multi-flue brick chimneys serving wood-burning fireplaces and heating boilers:
- **Freeze-Thaw Mortar Deterioration:** Water enters micro-cracks in aged lime mortar and porous brick masonry. As temperatures cycle between freezing and thawing dozens of times each winter, the expanding ice spalls the brick faces and splits step flashings.
- **Dead Valleys and Dormer Pinch Points:** Complex architectural designs, such as intersecting gables, shed dormers, and eyebrow windows, create "dead valleys" where snow and ice naturally accumulate. Without continuous, self-adhering waterproof membranes extending multiple feet up the vertical sidewalls, these junctions inevitably leak.

## Wisconsin Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Wis. Stat. Chapter 709 and the RECR

In Wisconsin, residential real estate transactions are strictly regulated by statutory disclosure frameworks designed to protect buyers from concealed defects.

### Wisconsin Statutes Chapter 709 and the Real Estate Condition Report (RECR)
Under Wisconsin Statutes § 709.01 through § 709.08, sellers of residential real estate (one to four units) must provide prospective buyers with a completed and signed **Real Estate Condition Report (RECR)** within 10 days of accepting an offer to purchase.

Wisconsin law defines a **"defect"** as a condition that would have a significant adverse effect on the value of the property, significantly impair the health or safety of future occupants, or shorten the normal life of the premises if not repaired.

Section B of the Wisconsin RECR specifically addresses structural elements:
- **Roof Age and Condition:** Does the seller know of any defects in the roof, including leaks, defective shingles, or improper installation?
- **Moisture Intrusion and Attic Dampness:** Does the seller know of any moisture infiltration, water accumulation, or dampness in the attic or ceilings?
- **Structural Framing:** Are there any known defects in the structural support components, including roof trusses, rafters, or load-bearing beams?

### Buyer's Statutory Right of Rescission
Wisconsin provides buyers with one of the most powerful consumer remedies in the country:
- **Two-Day Rescission Right:** If the seller fails to provide the RECR within 10 days of contract acceptance, or if the RECR reveals an undisclosed material defect, the buyer has a statutory right under Wis. Stat. § 709.05 to unilaterally rescind the purchase contract within two business days and receive a full refund of their earnest money deposit.
- **The "Band-Aid" Defense:** Sellers often attempt to sidestep disclosure liability by applying temporary cosmetic fixes—such as installing electric de-icing heat tape along the eaves or painting over attic water stains with stain-blocking primer—and subsequently checking "No" to defects on the RECR. Under Wisconsin common law fraud and Wis. Stat. § 100.18 (fraudulent representations), intentional concealment of known roof leaks exposes the seller to punitive damages and attorney's fees.

## The Manual Review Problem: Navigating Madison Disclosure Files

In standard South Central Wisconsin MLS (SCWMLS) contracts, buyers typically negotiate a 15-day inspection contingency. However, Madison real estate transactions present unique due diligence challenges.

A typical disclosure package in Dane County includes:
1. The 6-page Wisconsin Real Estate Condition Report.
2. A 40-to-60-page comprehensive home inspection report.
3. City of Madison Building Inspection Division historical permit archives.
4. Dane County Register of Deeds filings, including local historic preservation restrictions if located in districts like Third Lake Ridge, Mansion Hill, or University Heights.
5. Invoices or warranties from previous insulation, HVAC, or roofing contractors.

The critical danger in Madison lies in seasonal inspection limitations: **during winter months, snow accumulation frequently prevents inspectors from walking the roof or examining shingles**. An inspector's report may contain a brief disclaimer on page 4 stating: *"Roof covering not visible due to 8 inches of snowpack; visual inspection limited to attic space."* If the buyer relies on a pristine-looking RECR that conceals chronic ice dam leaks, they assume catastrophic financial risk.

### The Purchase
Peter and Emily purchased a charming 1926 Craftsman bungalow on a tree-lined street near Monroe Street and Lake Wingra for $535,000. The property featured original oak built-ins, stained glass windows, and a prominent front dormer roofline.

On the Wisconsin Real Estate Condition Report, the seller marked "No" to roof defects and wrote in the notes: *"Electric roof de-icing heat cables installed along front eave gutters in 2022; attic insulation upgraded; no current leaks."* The winter home inspection occurred in January when four inches of snow coated the shingles. The inspector noted from inside the attic that the insulation was deep and dry, but could not view the exterior decking. Peter and Emily waived their inspection contingency and finalized the purchase.

### The Disaster
Late the following February, Madison experienced a classic weather whiplash: an Arctic blast dropped temperatures to -12°F, followed three days later by a rapid warmup to 48°F with two inches of driving rain.

The electric heat cables failed to handle the volume of meltwater, which pooled behind a massive three-foot ice dam that bridged the dormer eaves. Within 48 hours, water began pouring through the original tongue-and-groove lath and plaster ceiling of the primary bedroom and streaming into the dining room below. When an emergency historic preservation contractor removed the shingles and opened the dormer wall, the true extent of the damage was revealed:
- The previous owner had nailed architectural shingles over rotted 100-year-old pine skip-sheathing without tearing off decayed wood.
- No self-adhering ice-and-water underlayment had been installed along the eaves or valleys, violating City of Madison building codes.
- The 2022 "insulation upgrade" had blown loose cellulose insulation directly over the attic soffit vents without installing baffles, completely blocking attic ventilation and exacerbating roof heat buildup.
- Six structural rafters supporting the front dormer had suffered extensive fungal wet rot and were actively bowing under load.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in Madison

### 1. Are electric heat cables an acceptable permanent solution for ice dams in Madison?
No. De-icing heat cables are merely a temporary symptom manager, not a cure for ice damming. Heat cables consume significant electrical power, frequently fail during extreme sub-zero cold snaps, and can degrade shingle asphalt over time. The only permanent solution for Madison homes is addressing the root causes: sealing attic air bypasses (chases, can lights, top plates), adding adequate attic insulation (at least R-49 to R-60), and ensuring balanced soffit-to-ridge attic ventilation.

### 2. Does the City of Madison require building permits for roof replacements?
Yes. The City of Madison Building Inspection Division requires a building permit for residential roof replacements, structural rafter repairs, or re-decking. Additionally, if the property is located in an officially designated City of Madison Historic District (such as Mansion Hill, Third Lake Ridge, or University Heights), any exterior modifications must be reviewed and approved by the Madison Landmarks Commission to ensure historic preservation compliance.

### 3. What ice barrier underlayment is required under Wisconsin building codes?
Under the Wisconsin Uniform Dwelling Code (UDC SPS 321.28), in regions subject to severe winter weather, residential roofs must have a self-adhering ice-and-water barrier underlayment installed along the eaves. The code mandates that this ice barrier must extend from the lowest roof edge up the slope to a point at least 24 inches inside the interior heated wall line of the building. Omission of this barrier is a common construction defect found in amateur re-roofs.

### 4. How should a buyer inspect a roof during a winter Madison home purchase?
If snowpack or icy conditions prevent the inspector from physically mounting the roof, buyers should never simply rely on the seller's RECR. Request that the purchase agreement include an extended inspection contingency allowing a specialized exterior roof re-inspection once the snow melts, or retain a contractor who can use thermal imaging cameras inside the attic to detect hidden moisture pockets and heat bypasses along the roof deck.
