## Why Des Moines Roofs Fail: Derecho Winds, Severe Hail, and Winter Ice Dams

Understanding why residential roofs deteriorate rapidly in Des Moines requires examining the severe atmospheric extremes of Central Iowa.

### Midwest Derecho and Straight-Line Wind Trauma
Des Moines sits squarely within the storm corridor frequently impacted by powerful convective complexes, including notorious Midwest derechos:
- **Severe Adhesive Seal Breakdown:** Straight-line winds gusting between 70 and 100 mph create intense aerodynamic negative pressure (uplift) across roof ridges, rakes, and eaves. These winds snap the factory thermal adhesive strips bonding shingles together. Once unsealed, shingles flap in the wind, causing creasing, tearing, and nail pull-through.
- **Micro-Tears and Water Channels:** Even if shingles remain attached to the roof deck, wind-creased shingle tabs allow wind-driven rain to blow horizontally underneath the shingles, bypassing the primary water barrier.

### High-Impact Hail and Latent Shingle Damage
Spring and summer thunderstorms in Polk County frequently produce large, dense hail:
- **Fiberglass Mat Fracturing:** Hailstones measuring 1.25 inches or larger strike roofs at high speeds, dislodging the protective mineral granules that shield the asphalt from ultraviolet degradation. Crucially, the impact often shatters the internal fiberglass reinforcement mat.
- **Delayed Leakage:** Hail impact bruises are often invisible from the ground and may not leak immediately. Over the following 12 to 24 months, solar radiation breaks down the exposed asphalt core, leading to premature cracking, water penetration, and widespread deck rot.

### Sub-Zero Freeze-Thaw and Eave Ice Damming
Des Moines winters feature prolonged stretches of sub-zero temperatures paired with heavy snowfall:
- **Attic Heat Loss and Ice Dam Formation:** In older homes throughout Beaverdale and Waveland Park, warmth escaping from heated living areas into poorly insulated attics melts the snow layer resting directly on the roof deck. The meltwater flows down the slope until it reaches the unheated roof eaves and gutters, where it promptly freezes.
- **Upstream Water Infiltration:** As ice builds up into a solid dam along the eave line, subsequent meltwater backs up behind the barrier. This pooled water forces its way backward underneath the shingle courses, penetrating roofing underlayment, soaking fascia boards, and cascading down interior plaster walls.

## Iowa Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Understanding Chapter 558A

Iowa law provides explicit statutory protections governing property condition disclosures in residential transactions.

### The Mandatory Iowa Seller Disclosure Statement (Iowa Code § 558A.2)
Under Iowa Code Chapter 558A, a person who offers to transfer residential real property consisting of one to four dwelling units must deliver a written **Iowa Residential Property Seller Disclosure Statement** to prospective buyers prior to an offer being accepted:
- **Section 6: Structural and Roof Disclosures:** The statutory disclosure form promulgated by the Iowa Real Estate Commission (IREC) specifically requires the seller to disclose:
  - The approximate age of the roof.
  - Any known physical defects in the roof, flashing, or gutters.
  - Any history of leaks, water seepage, or moisture accumulation.
  - Details regarding any past repairs, patches, or replacements performed during the seller's ownership.
- **Section 1: General Property Inquiries:** Sellers must disclose any past insurance claims related to property damage, including wind or hail damage.

### Seller Liability and the Good Faith Standard (Iowa Code § 558A.6)
Under Iowa Code Section 558A.6, a seller who fails to deliver the required disclosure statement or who knowingly provides false or misleading information is liable to the purchaser for actual damages:
- **Good Faith Requirement:** The disclosure must be made in good faith. If the seller had actual knowledge of prior roof leaks, failed repairs, or severe storm damage and concealed it, the buyer can recover the cost of complete roof replacement and repair of collateral damage.
- **No Liability for Latent Unknown Defects:** If a defect was unknown to the seller and could not have been discovered through the exercise of ordinary care, the seller is not liable under Chapter 558A. This makes obtaining an independent, professional roof inspection critical.

## The Manual Review Problem: Sifting Through Des Moines Disclosures

In Central Iowa residential transactions governed by the standard **Iowa Association of REALTORS® (IAR)** purchase agreement, buyers negotiate an **Inspection Contingency Period**—typically 10 to 14 calendar days.

During this brief window, buyers face a demanding stack of paperwork:
1. The mandatory Iowa Chapter 558A Seller Disclosure Statement.
2. A 40-to-60-page private home inspection report with routine disclaimers (e.g., *"Roof inspected from ground level due to steep 10/12 pitch and frost on shingles; decking integrity not verified"*).
3. City of Des Moines Permit and Development Center historical permit records for re-roofing or structural alterations.
4. Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (CLUE) insurance reports showing prior wind/hail claims.
5. Invoices or warranties from previous roofing contractors that may contain non-transferable fine print.

Sifting through these documents under tight contingency deadlines is risky. A seller might check "No" to active roof leaks while noting: *"Gutter repaired after 2023 storm."* In reality, that minor gutter repair masked a chronic ice dam problem that caused severe water damage inside exterior wall cavities.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in Des Moines

### 1. Does the City of Des Moines require a building permit for roof replacement?
Yes. The City of Des Moines Permit and Development Center requires a building permit for all residential re-roofing projects when stripping down to the decking or replacing structural sheathing. Inspections ensure compliance with the International Residential Code (IRC), including mandatory ice barrier installation along eaves.

### 2. What constitutes an "Ice Barrier" requirement under Des Moines building codes?
Under the IRC as adopted in Iowa, an approved ice barrier—typically a self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen membrane (ice-and-water shield)—must be installed along all roof eaves. The membrane must extend from the roof edge to a point at least 24 inches inside the interior heated wall line of the building to prevent water intrusion from ice dams.

### 3. How do Class 4 impact-resistant shingles benefit Des Moines homeowners?
Given Central Iowa's vulnerability to severe hailstorms, installing Underwriters Laboratories (UL) 2218 Class 4 impact-resistant shingles is highly recommended. These shingles are engineered to withstand the impact of 2-inch steel balls dropped from 20 feet without cracking the underlying mat. Many major property insurance carriers offer substantial annual premium discounts (often 10% to 25%) for homes with certified Class 4 roofs.

### 4. What should a buyer do if an Iowa seller had a prior roof insurance claim?
If a seller discloses or a CLUE report reveals a prior insurance claim for wind or hail damage, the buyer should demand copies of the insurance adjuster’s scope of loss and contractor paid receipts. This verifies whether the full claim payout was actually invested into professional repairs or whether the seller merely pocketed the funds and performed cosmetic patches.
