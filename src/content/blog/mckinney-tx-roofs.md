## Why McKinney Roofs Fail: Hail Impacts, 105°F Thermal Baking, and Straight-Line Winds

Roofs in Collin County encounter a punishing combination of mechanical impacts and intense environmental weathering that degrades shingles far ahead of manufacturer projections.

### Hail Impact Mechanics: Bruising, Fracturing, and Granule Loss
Hail is the single greatest cause of residential roof failure across McKinney and North Texas:
- **Velocity and Kinetic Energy:** Hailstones traveling at terminal velocities between 50 and 90 mph strike roof planes with crushing force.
- **Sub-Surface Bruising:** While a shingle may not show a visible puncture immediately, the impact crushes the underlying fiberglass matting. By pressing on the impact point, an inspector can feel the characteristic spongy "bruise," indicating that the structural integrity of the shingle is broken.
- **Granule Dislodgement:** Hail impacts blast away the protective ceramic mineral granules, exposing the black, petroleum-based asphalt substrate beneath.
- **Accelerated UV Oxidation:** Under Texas’s blistering summer sun, exposed asphalt dries, oxidizes, and shrinks within months, creating microscopic fractures that allow rainwater to seep down to the roof decking during subsequent downpours.

### The 160°F Attic Kiln: Shingle Baking and Thermal Shock
McKinney experiences extended summer heatwaves where outdoor temperatures top 100°F for weeks:
- **Thermal Breakdown:** Inadequate attic ventilation turns attics into ovens, driving roof deck temperatures past 160°F. This internal heat bakes shingles from beneath, evaporating volatile plasticizers and causing shingles to curl, cup, and blister.
- **Rapid Thermal Cycling:** During spring and autumn "blue northers," temperatures in McKinney can plunge 40°F in two hours following a thunderstorm. This rapid contraction stresses flashing joints around stone chimneys, brick parapets, and plumbing vent pipes, tearing caulking and popping nails.

### High-Wind Uplift and Creased Shingles
Severe North Texas thunderstorms routinely produce straight-line wind gusts between 60 and 80 mph:
- **Sealant Strip Delamination:** Wind uplift breaks the factory adhesive sealant strips connecting shingle tabs.
- **Creasing and Tearing:** Flapping shingles bend along their upper nail line, creating horizontal stress creases that tear the fiberglass mat and allow water penetration.

## Understanding Texas Real Estate Disclosure Laws

Texas law provides robust consumer protections regarding property condition disclosures while imposing substantial penalties on sellers who conceal known defects.

### Texas Property Code § 5.008: The Seller's Disclosure Notice
Under **Texas Property Code § 5.008**, a seller of a single-unit residential real property must deliver a written notice of property condition. The statutory notice, standardized by the **Texas Real Estate Commission (TREC Form OP-H)** or the Texas REALTORS® (TXR 1406) disclosure form, contains critical questions regarding the roof:
- **Section 4 / Roof Condition:** The seller must disclose the roof type (e.g., composition shingle, tile, metal), approximate age, whether the seller is aware of any known defects, and whether the roof has been repaired during ownership.
- **Insurance Claims History:** Under Texas REALTORS® disclosures, sellers are specifically asked whether they have filed any insurance claims for hail, wind, or water damage, and whether claim proceeds were actually used to complete the required repairs.
- **Water Penetration History:** Sellers must disclose any history of roof leaks, water penetration into ceilings or walls, or uncorrected structural moisture problems.

### Buyer's Statutory Right to Terminate Under Texas Property Code § 5.008(f)
Texas law provides an absolute termination remedy if the disclosure notice is not handled strictly:
- If the seller fails to deliver the Seller's Disclosure Notice on or before the effective date of the purchase contract, the buyer has the statutory right to **terminate the contract for any reason within seven days** after receiving the notice, or at any time prior to closing if the notice was never delivered, obtaining an immediate refund of earnest money.

### The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA)
Texas enforces powerful remedies against sellers who actively conceal property defects:
- Under the **Texas Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act (DTPA, Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 17.41 et seq.)**, a seller or broker who engages in false, misleading, or deceptive acts—such as failing to disclose known roof hail damage or pocketing insurance proceeds while performing cheap cosmetic patches—can be sued for **treble damages** (three times actual economic damages) plus court costs and mandatory attorney fees.

## The Manual Review Problem: Navigating Collin County Escrow Documents

In McKinney, an escrow transaction packet frequently runs 175 to 250 pages, comprising:
- The TREC or TXR Seller’s Disclosure Notice.
- Comprehensive HOA resale certificates and architectural guidelines in master-planned communities like Stonebridge Ranch and Craig Ranch.
- City of McKinney building permit histories.
- General home inspection reports, WDI (termite) inspection reports, and HVAC assessments.
- Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (C.L.U.E.) insurance loss reports detailing property claim histories.

### Ambiguous Phrases Used by Sellers to Obscure Roof Damage
Sellers wanting to protect their sales price without spending $18,000 to $30,000 on a reroof often use carefully constructed euphemisms:
- *"Roof inspected by roofer following spring 2023 hailstorm; certified functional."* (Hides that the roofer documented widespread hail bruising that totaled the roof, but the seller decided against replacing it).
- *"Minor ceiling drywall water spot in bonus room repainted during recent remodel."* (Conceals an uncorrected flashing failure along a multi-level dormer junction).
- *"Insurance claim filed in 2022 for wind; claim settled."* (Omits that the seller took a $16,000 payout from the insurance carrier and spent it on landscaping rather than replacing the damaged roof).
- *"Roof age: 8 years; Class 4 impact-resistant shingles."* (Fails to disclose that severe hail stripped granules off 40% of the south-facing slopes, invalidating impact ratings).

With Texas purchase contracts usually featuring tight **7-to-10-day option periods**, manually reviewing hundreds of pages while cross-referencing insurance histories creates extreme risk for buyers.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in McKinney

### 1. What are Class 4 impact-resistant shingles, and are they worth it in McKinney?
Class 4 shingles are roofing materials tested under UL 2218 standards, capable of withstanding a 2-inch steel ball dropped from 20 feet without cracking the backing. In Collin County’s violent hail climate, Class 4 shingles are highly recommended. Not only do they resist moderate hail without shattering, but most major Texas home insurance carriers also offer substantial annual premium discounts (ranging from 15% to 28%) for homes equipped with certified Class 4 roofs.

### 2. Can a Texas homebuyer verify if a previous roof insurance claim was filed?
Yes. Homebuyers should request a Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (C.L.U.E.) report or an insurance loss history report from the seller during the option period. This report details all property damage insurance claims filed on the address over the past five to seven years, showing payout amounts, dates of loss, and claim types (such as hail or water intrusion), allowing buyers to verify if claim funds were used for genuine roof replacements.

### 3. Does the City of McKinney require permits for residential reroofs?
Yes. The City of McKinney Chief Building Official requires a building permit for all residential roof replacements. Registered roofing contractors must pull a permit, and the work must comply with the current International Residential Code (IRC) adopted by the city, including requirements for drip edges, valley flashing, and proper attic ventilation ratios.

### 4. What happens if a seller pockets an insurance payout and fails to disclose it in Texas?
If a seller files an insurance claim, receives a settlement check to replace a hail-damaged roof, fails to replace the roof, and then sells the home without disclosing the unrepaired damage on the Seller's Disclosure Notice, the seller is subject to serious legal liability. Under the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA), the buyer can sue for triple economic damages, fraud, and attorney fees. Furthermore, the buyer's future insurance claims may be denied by carriers due to pre-existing damage.
