## The Hidden Threat: Termites in Corona and Western Riverside County

Corona’s geographical position at the base of the Santa Ana Mountains creates microclimates ranging from hot suburban valleys to exposed hillside ridges. These topographical characteristics foster vigorous colonies of destructive wood-destroying pests.

### Why Corona Housing Stock and Inland Empire Climate Elevate Pest Risk

A combination of construction techniques, climate phenomena, and landscaping habits elevates termite risk across Corona neighborhoods:

- **Santa Ana Wind Swarms:** In late summer and autumn, hot, dry Santa Ana winds sweep through the Corona corridor. These winds trigger extensive swarms of Western Drywood Termites, carrying alates (winged reproductive termites) directly into attic gable vents, eave overhangs, and uncaulked roof fascia.
- **Monolithic Slabs and Buried Weep Screeds:** The vast majority of suburban homes built in Eagle Glen, South Corona, and Sierra Del Oro from the 1980s onward feature slab-on-grade foundations. Homeowners and landscapers often pile planter soil, sod, and mulch above the exterior stucco weep screed. This allows subterranean termites to tunnel behind exterior stucco directly into wall studs without visible mud tubes on the foundation exterior.
- **Plumbing Penetrations and Trap Block-Outs:** Post-tension and standard concrete slabs contain cutouts beneath bathtubs and showers. Subterranean termites utilize these unsealed sub-slab voids to migrate directly into structural wall plates and partition timber.
- **Architectural Vulnerabilities Across Corona Neighborhoods:**
  - *Downtown & Grand Boulevard Historic District:* 1910s–1950s craftsman and bungalow homes with raised wood floors, unsealed crawl spaces, and direct wood-to-earth contact at porch steps and perimeter framing.
  - *Sierra Del Oro & Corona Hills:* 1980s–1990s two-story homes with complex rooflines, exposed wood rafter tails, and stucco pop-outs where drywood termites establish aerial colonies.
  - *Eagle Glen & Dos Lagos:* 2000s master-planned developments where expansive clay soils cause minor slab settling cracks that serve as subterranean termite access points.

### Pest Biology: Subterranean vs. Drywood Termites in Southern California

Homebuyers in Corona must understand the behavioral distinctions between two dominant termite species:

- **Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** Ground-dwelling insects that require soil moisture to survive. They construct earthen shelter tubes over concrete foundation stems to reach interior wood framing, consuming softwood along the grain and packing hollowed wood cavities with mud.
- **Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*):** Non-soil-dependent insects that inhabit dry, sound structural timber. They establish colonies directly inside attic roof trusses, window headers, and exterior rafter tails, carving smooth chambers across wood grain and pushing out distinctive hard, six-sided fecal pellets (frass).

## California Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Civil Code § 1102 and SPCB Branch 3 Reports

California maintains some of the nation's most stringent real estate disclosure statutes, requiring residential sellers to disclose all known material facts affecting the value and desirability of the property.

### The Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and SPQ

Under **California Civil Code § 1102 et seq.**, sellers must deliver a completed **Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)** and the California Association of Realtors (C.A.R.) **Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)**:

- **TDS Section II.C:** Inquires whether the seller is aware of *"Any settling, slippage, sliding, or other soil problems,"* or *"Flooding, drainage or grading problems,"* which frequently correlate with subterranean termite entry points.
- **SPQ Section V (Pests, Infestations, and Soil):** Specifically asks the seller:
  - *"Are you aware of any past or present pest infestations, including termites, ants, or wood-destroying pests?"*
  - *"Are you aware of any past or present termite or pest control treatments, reports, or warranties?"*
  - *"Are you aware of any repairs or alterations to the property due to pest damage?"*

Under California law, a seller who checks "No" while possessing knowledge of past swarms, localized chemical treatments, or pest inspection reports faces substantial liability for intentional fraud or negligent misrepresentation.

### Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) Branch 3 Reports: Section 1 vs. Section 2

In California, official Wood Destroying Pest and Organisms (WDO) inspection reports are conducted by **Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB)** licensed Branch 3 companies and categorize findings into two distinct sections:

- **Section 1 Findings (Active Infestations / Direct Damage):** Documents visible active subterranean or drywood termite colonies, wood rot/fungus, and actual structural damage requiring immediate treatment and structural repair.
- **Section 2 Conditions (Conducive Conditions):** Documents conditions deemed likely to lead to future infestation or decay, such as earth-to-wood contact, stucco weep screeds buried below exterior grade, faulty plumbing leaks, or cracked foundation sealants.

### What Corona Sellers Reveal vs. Strategically Conceal

Sellers in Western Riverside County often utilize euphemistic language to minimize significant pest damage on disclosure documents:
- *"Completed spot foam treatment for drywood termites in garage eaves in 2023."* (Conceals that spot treatment was chosen to avoid the expense of whole-home tent fumigation).
- *"Fascia board refreshed during exterior paint job."* (Downplays the fact that rotted, termite-hollowed rafter tails were merely covered with wood filler and fresh paint).
- *"Termite service contract kept on file."* (Fails to disclose that bait stations were placed around the perimeter after subterranean mud tubes were found in the master bathroom wall).

## The Manual Review Problem: Overlooking Crucial Clues in 200-Page Closing Packets

During California's standard 17-day buyer investigation period under the C.A.R. Residential Purchase Agreement (RPA), buyers must review an enormous compilation of technical paperwork:
- C.A.R. Residential Purchase Agreement and Contingency Addenda
- Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)
- 50-to-80-page Comprehensive General Home Inspection Report
- SPCB Branch 3 Structural Pest Control Report with Section 1 and Section 2 Work Estimates
- Riverside County Natural Hazard Disclosures (NHD), Preliminary Title Report, and HOA Disclosures

### The Danger of Disconnected Clues

Material structural hazards rarely show up as an explicit red flag in a single document. Instead, critical indicators are scattered across multiple disclosures:
- The Home Inspector notes on page 52: *"Minor stucco crack and high moisture reading near rear sliding door weep screed."*
- The SPCB Pest Inspector records under Inaccessible Areas: *"Exterior wall framing behind finished drywall and stucco inaccessible for visual inspection."*
- The Seller checks "Yes" to prior treatments on the SPQ, writing: *"Localized chemical barrier treatment completed in 2022."*

Individually, these notes seem innocuous. But synthesized together, they tell a perilous story: buried weep screeds allowed subterranean termites to bypass exterior inspection, crawl behind the stucco, and hollow out the rear load-bearing structural header above the sliding glass door.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Corona

### 1. Are termites a widespread problem in Corona, California?
Yes. Both Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*) and Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*) are extremely common throughout Corona and Riverside County. The combination of intense summer heat, hot Santa Ana wind swarms, and slab-on-grade construction creates constant pest pressure across both older and newer residential communities.

### 2. What are California sellers legally required to disclose regarding termites?
Under California Civil Code § 1102 and the standard Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ), sellers must disclose all known past or present termite infestations, pest damage, previous treatments, and copies of any pest control inspection reports or warranties in their possession.

### 3. What is the difference between Section 1 and Section 2 on a California Pest Report?
On an SPCB Branch 3 Pest Report, **Section 1** items represent active wood-destroying infestations or direct physical damage that requires immediate treatment or structural repair. **Section 2** items represent conducive conditions (such as earth-to-wood contact, buried stucco weep screeds, or moisture leaks) that do not currently have active infestation but could lead to one if left uncorrected.

### 4. Does California homeowners insurance cover termite repairs?
No. Standard California homeowners insurance policies strictly exclude damage caused by termites, dry rot, and insect infestations. Insurance carriers view termite damage as preventable wear-and-tear maintenance, leaving the property owner solely responsible for all structural reconstruction expenses.
