## Why Evansville Roofs Fail: Ohio River Microclimates, Squall Lines, and Algae Decay

Evansville’s low-lying position along the Ohio River basin creates a unique microclimate characterized by high ambient humidity, intense seasonal convective storms, and sharp temperature fluctuations that degrade residential roofing assemblies.

### Severe Convective Thunderstorms and Straight-Line Microbursts
The tri-state region sits directly within an active severe convective storm corridor:
- **Wind Uplift and Tab Tearing:** Spring and summer cold fronts colliding with humid Gulf air generate ferocious squall lines and straight-line microbursts with winds exceeding 70 to 80 mph. These gusts create intense negative pressure (uplift) along roof ridges, eaves, and windward rakes.
- **Sealant Strip Delamination:** Over years of exposure to Evansville’s summer humidity and sun, factory-applied asphalt adhesive strips lose their bond strength. Once broken, high winds flutter the unbonded shingle tabs, creasing the fiberglass mat along the fastener line until shingles tear free and expose the underlying underlayment to torrential downpours.
- **Hail Bruising and Sub-Surface Fractures:** Hailstones measuring 1.25 to 2.25 inches frequently bombard Vanderburgh County. Impacts crush the protective mineral granules, leaving the underlying asphalt compound vulnerable to rapid solar oxidation. Beneath the surface, impact kinetic energy shatters the fiberglass reinforcement mat, forming concealed micro-fissures that leak during subsequent winter storms.

### *Gloeocapsa magma* Algae and Biological Moss Colonization
The Ohio River Valley’s sustained summer humidity creates an ideal environment for biological degradation:
- **Cyanobacteria Infestation (*Gloeocapsa magma*):** The dark black streaks commonly observed on Evansville roofs are colonies of *Gloeocapsa magma*, a photosynthetic cyanobacteria that feeds on the calcium carbonate limestone filler used in modern asphalt composition shingles.
- **Loss of Reflectivity and Thermal Stress:** As algae colonies spread, they darken the roof surface, drastically reducing solar reflectance. Shingle surface temperatures surge past 160°F, accelerating asphalt binder volatilization and leaving shingles brittle and curled.
- **Moss Damming and Capillary Action:** In shaded East Side and Lincolnshire neighborhoods with dense oak and maple canopies, green moss and lichens take root in shingle keyways. Moss dams retain rainwater against shingle edges, wicking water upward through capillary action and rotting structural wood decking beneath.

### Rapid Freeze-Thaw Cycles and Eave Ice Damming
While Southwestern Indiana experiences milder winters than northern parts of the state, Evansville experiences frequent freeze-thaw cycling:
- **Thermal Inversion and Snow Melt:** Temperatures frequently hover around the freezing mark (30°F to 35°F) during winter storms. Poorly insulated attics in mid-century East Side ranches allow heat to escape, melting the snow blanket on upper roof planes.
- **Eave Refreezing:** Meltwater running down to unheated eaves refreezes into thick ice bands. Water ponds behind these dams and forces its way beneath shingle courses, saturating attic insulation and rotting exterior wall framing.

### Unpermitted Multi-Layer Shingle Overlays
To avoid the expense of tearing off old roofing, property owners in Evansville often install a second layer of shingles directly over failing ones:
- **Trapped Moisture and Heat:** A second shingle layer traps solar heat and condensation, rapidly cooking both layers of shingles and cutting their lifespan in half.
- **Dead-Load Overload:** Two layers of heavy architectural shingles add thousands of pounds of dead weight to aging 2x4 or 2x6 rafters, leading to noticeable roof sagging, rafter deflection, and cracked interior plaster ceilings.

## Understanding Indiana Real Estate Disclosure Laws

Indiana enforces statutory consumer disclosure standards to ensure prospective purchasers receive clear, truthful information regarding a home’s structural condition.

### The Indiana Residential Real Estate Sales Disclosure Act (Ind. Code § 32-21-5)
Under **Indiana Code § 32-21-5-7**, single-family residential sellers must deliver a completed **Seller's Residential Real Estate Sales Disclosure Form (State Form 46234 / Form R-1)** to the prospective buyer prior to contract acceptance.

Section C ("Property Systems & Structure") of Form 46234 requires explicit disclosures concerning the roof:
- **Roof Age and Covering:** The seller must state the type of roof covering (e.g., composition shingle, metal, rubber, tile) and its approximate age.
- **Known Leaks and Deficiencies:** The seller must disclose whether there are any known leaks, moisture penetration, or structural defects in the roof, flashings, or gutters.
- **Prior Repair History:** If repairs have been performed, the seller must detail the dates, extent, and contractor identity for all maintenance work.
- **Unpermitted Work:** The seller must affirmatively disclose any structural modifications or reroofing performed without required municipal building permits.

### Caveat Emptor Exceptions and Landmark Case Law (*Johnson v. Wysocki*)
While Indiana courts historically applied the doctrine of *caveat emptor* ("buyer beware"), current Indiana jurisprudence strictly penalizes sellers who make false representations on disclosure forms:
- In ***Johnson v. Wysocki*, 990 N.E.2d 456 (Ind. 2013)**, the Indiana Supreme Court established that a seller can be held liable for fraudulent misrepresentation or active concealment if they had actual knowledge of a defect and failed to disclose it on Form 46234, or if they affirmatively misrepresented the condition of the property.
- The court held that an "as-is" clause in a standard purchase agreement does not shield a seller from liability for fraudulent misrepresentation on statutory disclosure forms.
- If a seller knowingly conceals roof leaks behind cosmetic painting or lies about uncompleted storm damage repairs, the buyer can sue for compensatory damages to restore the roof, as well as punitive damages and attorney's fees in cases of egregious fraud.

### Vanderburgh County / City of Evansville Building Permits
The **Evansville-Vanderburgh County Building Commission** enforces the Indiana Residential Code (675 IAC 14 / 2018 IRC with Indiana amendments):
- **Permit Requirement:** A residential building permit is legally mandatory for any reroofing project involving full replacement or covering more than 100 square feet.
- **Maximum Number of Overlays:** Indiana code strictly prohibits installing a new roof covering over more than one existing layer of roofing. If two layers already exist, or if the underlying decking is rotted or damaged, a complete tear-off down to structural sheathing is required.
- **Decking Standards:** Building inspectors require that all broken, rotted, or delaminated plywood and spaced board sheathing be replaced before installing new underlayment.

## The Manual Review Problem: Navigating Indiana Due Diligence

Under standard Indiana Association of Realtors (IAR) purchase agreements, buyers typically negotiate an **Inspection Period of 10 to 14 calendar days**.

During this narrow due diligence window, prospective buyers must evaluate multiple complex documents:
- The completed Indiana State Form 46234 disclosure document.
- C.L.U.E. insurance loss history reports showing previous wind and hail claims on the property.
- Building permit logs from the Evansville-Vanderburgh County Building Commission.
- General home inspection reports spanning 45 to 65 pages that frequently disclaim comprehensive roof evaluations due to roof height, pitch, or wet surface conditions.

### Vague Phrasing Sellers Use to Disguise Roof Damage
Sellers in Evansville frequently employ evasive language on disclosure forms to minimize serious roofing defects and avoid paying for a costly replacement:
- *"Roof inspected annually by reputable local contractor; minor shingle maintenance completed."* (Conceals that a contractor confirmed widespread hail mat bruising and urged full tear-off).
- *"Aesthetic dark algae staining present; shingles structurally sound."* (Masks extensive granule loss, surface pitting, and dried-out, curled shingles).
- *"Small attic water mark from historic storm; area dry and sealed."* (Disguises an active valley flashing failure that leaks during sustained river squalls).
- *"New architectural shingles installed in 2022."* (Hides that the shingles were installed as an unpermitted second layer over rotted organic sheathing).

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in Evansville, IN

### 1. Does the City of Evansville require a building permit for roof replacement?
Yes. The Evansville-Vanderburgh County Building Commission requires a residential building permit for all reroofing projects exceeding 100 square feet or involving a complete tear-off down to the decking. Installations must comply with the Indiana Residential Code (675 IAC 14). Inspectors evaluate wood decking condition and ensure that no more than two layers of roofing exist on the structure.

### 2. Can a seller in Indiana be held liable for hiding roof leaks?
Yes. Under Indiana Code § 32-21-5 and the Indiana Supreme Court's ruling in *Johnson v. Wysocki*, sellers are legally liable for fraudulent misrepresentation if they fail to disclose known material defects on State Form 46234. If a seller conceals water leaks behind cosmetic paint or fails to disclose known structural failures, an "as-is" contract clause does not shield them from liability, and buyers can sue for full repair damages and attorney's fees.

### 3. What are the dark streaks on roofs in Evansville, and do they cause damage?
The dark streaks are airborne colonies of *Gloeocapsa magma*, a photosynthetic cyanobacteria that feeds on the limestone filler in asphalt shingles. While often dismissed as purely cosmetic, severe algae infestations darken the roof, causing shingles to absorb excessive heat. This accelerates asphalt oil loss, promotes granule shedding, and eventually leads to premature shingle curling and cracking.

### 4. Is it legal in Indiana to install a new roof over old shingles?
Indiana building codes permit a maximum of two layers of roof coverings. However, installing an overlay over damaged, cupped, or rotted shingles is a code violation. Furthermore, multi-layer roofs trap excess heat, void many manufacturer shingle warranties, and add substantial dead-load weight to older roof trusses, making a complete tear-off the industry-recommended standard.
