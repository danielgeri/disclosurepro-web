## The Hidden Threat: Termites Across Columbia and the Midlands

Columbia is situated in the central Midlands along the fall line where the Piedmont plateau meets the Atlantic Coastal Plain, anchored by the confluence of the Saluda, Broad, and Congaree Rivers. These river basin conditions create persistent, high-humidity soil environments that foster aggressive wood-destroying organism activity year-round.

### How Midlands Heat, Crawlspaces, and Historic Framing Exacerbate Infestations

Several regional construction and environmental factors make Columbia properties exceptionally susceptible to termite infestations:

- **Humid Subtropical River Basin Climate:** Columbia experiences intense, prolonged summer heatwaves combined with humidity levels regularly exceeding 80%. Frequent afternoon convective thunderstorms saturate the soil. These warm, persistently moist soil conditions allow subterranean termite colonies to forage actively twelve months a year without winter dormancy.
- **Traditional Pier-and-Beam Crawlspace Construction:** A vast majority of historic and mid-century homes in Shandon, Rosewood, and Elmwood are built on unconditioned, vented pier-and-beam crawlspaces. Warm, humid outdoor air enters the cooler crawlspace through foundation vents, condenses on wood framing, and drives wood moisture content well above the 20% threshold required for wood-decay fungi and subterranean termites to flourish.
- **Heart Pine and Douglas Fir Dimensional Lumber:** Historic Columbia homes built between 1900 and 1940 feature dense heart pine and oak framing. While heart pine exhibits natural resistance, sapwood framing and floor sills that have suffered decades of ground moisture absorption become prime targets for subterranean termites.
- **The Spread of Formosan "Super Termites" into the Midlands:** Historically confined to coastal areas like Charleston, Formosan Subterranean Termites (*Coptotermes formosanus*) have migrated inland into Richland and Lexington Counties, transported via infested landscape timbers, pine straw mulch, and recycled railroad ties. Formosan colonies are exponentially larger and more destructive than native species, capable of devouring structural framing at alarming speeds.
- **Mulch and Landscape Bedding against Brick Piers:** In Columbia’s historic districts, lush landscape beds, organic hardwood mulch, and dense azalea bushes are frequently planted directly against brick foundation piers. Mulch traps moisture and provides an unbroken bridge of cellulose directly from the soil into floor framing.

### Pest Biology: Eastern Subterranean vs. Formosan Subterranean Termites

Columbia homebuyers must understand the two primary subterranean termite threats:

1. **Eastern Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes flavipes*):** The most common native pest in the Midlands. These soil-dwelling insects live in underground colonies of 200,000 to 1,000,000 individuals. They construct mud shelter tubes across masonry foundation piers, brick stem walls, and plumbing stacks to access wood subfloors, eating the softer springwood grain from the inside out.
2. **Formosan Subterranean Termites (*Coptotermes formosanus*):** Known as "super termites," Formosan colonies can contain over 5 to 10 million individuals. Unlike native species, Formosans can establish aerial nests (cartons) within wall cavities using moisture from condensation or plumbing leaks, allowing them to survive without soil contact. They feed voraciously on wood, sheetrock paper, and even chew through non-cellulose materials such as PVC pipes and foam insulation to reach framing.

## South Carolina Real Estate Disclosure Laws: S.C. Code Ann. § 27-50-10 and the CL-100

South Carolina enforces strict statutory disclosure mandates designed to protect real estate purchasers from undisclosed latent property defects.

### The Statutory Framework: S.C. Code Ann. § 27-50-10 et seq.

Under the **South Carolina Residential Property Condition Disclosure Act (S.C. Code Ann. § 27-50-10 et seq.)**, sellers of residential real estate must provide a standardized **Property Condition Disclosure Statement** approved by the South Carolina Real Estate Commission (SCREC):

- **Section 2: Roof, Foundation, and Structural Components:** Sellers must disclose known defects, structural settling, crawlspace moisture problems, or framing damage.
- **Section 4: Environmental and Pest Inquiries:** Sellers must explicitly disclose:
  - Any known past or active infestations of termites, dry rot, wood-decay fungi, or wood-destroying beetles.
  - Prior chemical treatments, subterranean baiting systems, or structural repairs performed to correct insect damage.
  - Whether there is an active, transferable termite bond or warranty covering the structure.
- **Liability for Misrepresentation under S.C. Code Ann. § 27-50-65:** A seller who knowingly violates disclosure obligations or provides false, incomplete, or misleading information is liable to the buyer for actual damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees. 

### The Official South Carolina Wood Infestation Report (Form CL-100)

In South Carolina real estate practice, the **CL-100 Report** is the definitive legal document governing wood-destroying organisms. Regulated by the **Clemson University Department of Pesticide Regulation**, the CL-100 is typically required by mortgage lenders within 30 days of closing.

The CL-100 evaluates four distinct categories:
1. Visible active infestation of subterranean termites.
2. Visible prior infestation of subterranean termites (with an evaluation of whether structural damage was repaired).
3. Other wood-destroying insects (powder-post beetles, wood borers).
4. Wood-decay fungi and moisture conditions in crawlspaces (wood moisture content exceeding 20% is flagged as conducive to decay and infestation).

### What Columbia Sellers Strategically Reveal vs. Conceal

In competitive Columbia transactions, sellers frequently obscure termite history through deceptive wording:
- *"Active termite bond in place with local pest company; renewable annually."* (Sellers often tout a "termite bond" as complete protection, concealing that the bond is an *inspection-only* or *re-treatment-only* bond that provides zero coverage for structural repairs).
- *"Previous crawlspace moisture barrier installed in 2021."* (Masks the fact that moisture levels exceeded 28% and caused extensive wood-decay rot and termite activity along the main support girder).
- *"Subfloor cosmetically leveled during recent interior renovations."* (Frequently hides that floor sills were hollowed out by termites, and the seller simply added subfloor shims and new luxury vinyl plank flooring instead of replacing structural lumber).

## The Manual Review Problem: Overlooking Critical Red Flags in 200-Page Closings

During a Columbia purchase transaction, buyers typically have a contractual Due Diligence period (often 10 to 14 days under standard South Carolina Association of Realtors contracts). During this period, buyers are presented with an overwhelming volume of legal and technical documents:

- The South Carolina Agreement to Buy and Sell Real Estate and financing addenda.
- The statutory SCREC Property Condition Disclosure Statement.
- A 60-to-90-page general home inspection report filled with photographs.
- The official South Carolina CL-100 Wood Infestation Report and termite bond paperwork.
- Title insurance binders, county property surveys, and HOA covenants.

### The Deceptive Nuance of "Prior Damage with No Active Pests"

The most dangerous scenario on a South Carolina CL-100 report is a notation stating:
- *"Visible evidence of prior subterranean termite infestation observed at crawlspace band board; no active live termites observed at time of inspection."*

When buyers see "no active termites," they often assume the issue is resolved and sign off on the report. However, without a licensed structural engineer or specialized framing contractor evaluating the depth of wood loss, the home could have load-bearing floor girders and sills hollowed out to within 10% of their structural capacity.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Columbia

### 1. What is a South Carolina CL-100 report and is it mandatory in Columbia?
A CL-100 report (Official South Carolina Wood Infestation Report) is a specialized structural pest inspection report governed by the Clemson University Department of Pesticide Regulation. While South Carolina state law does not mandate a CL-100 for private cash sales, nearly all institutional mortgage lenders require a clear CL-100 report completed within 30 days of closing before funding a loan. It inspects for active termites, prior termite damage, other wood-destroying insects, and crawlspace fungal decay/moisture.

### 2. What is the difference between subterranean termites and Formosan termites in the Midlands?
Native Eastern Subterranean Termites live underground and construct mud tubes to reach wood framing, feeding relatively slowly over years. Formosan Subterranean Termites—an invasive species now established across Richland and Lexington Counties—form colonies numbering in the millions. Formosans can create above-ground nests (cartons) without soil contact, consume wood up to 10 times faster than native species, and can severely compromise a home's structural framing in just a few months.

### 3. Can a seller in Columbia be sued for failing to disclose termite history on the SCREC disclosure?
Yes. Under S.C. Code Ann. § 27-50-65, a seller who knowingly fails to disclose known active infestations, prior termite damage, or past treatments on the SCREC Property Condition Disclosure Statement is liable to the buyer for actual structural damages, court costs, and reasonable attorney's fees. In cases of intentional fraud or concealment, buyers may also pursue common-law claims for fraudulent misrepresentation and punitive damages.

### 4. How should a buyer interpret a CL-100 report that lists "previous infestation with no active visible organisms"?
A finding of "previous infestation with no active visible organisms" is a major warning flag. While chemical treatments may have eradicated live insects, the report does NOT certify that the remaining structural framing is structurally sound. Columbia buyers should immediately hire an independent licensed residential builder or structural engineer to probe damaged floor joists, sills, and girders to determine if load-bearing capacity has been compromised.
