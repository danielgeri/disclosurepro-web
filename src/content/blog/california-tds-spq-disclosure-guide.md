If you are buying a home in California, you won't just receive a single disclosure form—you will likely receive a **100- to 250-page disclosure packet** delivered via Glide, Disclosures.io, or DocuSign, often with only a few days to review before submitting a competitive offer or removing contingencies.

Knowing which pages carry the highest financial risk—and where sellers routinely bury expensive defects—can save you **$15,000 to $50,000+** in post-closing surprises or repair credit negotiations.

---

## The 5 Core Documents Inside a California Disclosure Packet

Under **California Civil Code § 1102**, sellers of 1–4 unit residential properties must complete statutory disclosures even when marketing a home "as-is." Most packets contain five critical components:

### 1. Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)
The **TDS** is California's mandatory statutory form. Pay sharpest attention to **Section II (Seller's Information)**, where the seller checks boxes for structural components, roof, plumbing, electrical, foundation, drainage, and room additions, followed by handwritten or typed explanations.
- **Red flag pattern:** A checked box paired with minimized phrasing like *"minor seasonal seepage in crawlspace—managed with sump pump"* or *"hairline stucco cracks typical for age."* Always cross-check every "Yes" box against the general home inspection and invoices at the back of the packet.

### 2. Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)
The C.A.R. **SPQ** asks **50+ granular follow-up questions** that the TDS doesn't cover in detail—including insurance claims filed within the past 5 years, past water intrusion or mold remediation, unpermitted conversions, neighborhood noise/nuisances, and HOA disputes.
- **Red flag pattern:** Any mention of a **prior homeowner's insurance claim** (especially water damage or roof claims) can impact your ability to bind affordable coverage in California's tight insurance market (or force you onto the California FAIR Plan).

### 3. Agent Visual Inspection Disclosure (AVID)
California law requires both the listing agent and buyer's agent to walk the property and document visible defects room-by-room on an **AVID**.
- **Why it matters:** Listing agents want to shield their brokerage from post-closing lawsuits, so they frequently note observations on the AVID (*"uneven flooring in hallway," "staining at garage ceiling," "sloping patio slab"*) that the seller left completely blank on the TDS! Always compare the seller's TDS against the listing agent's AVID for contradictions.

### 4. Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD) Report
Third-party NHD reports commonly span **40 to 70 pages** of boilerplate maps and statutory text, causing most buyers to skip them completely. Jump straight to the **Summary Signature Page** near the front to check six state-level zones:
1. **Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zone (VHFHSZ)** or State Responsibility Area (major wildfire insurance cost impact)
2. **FEMA Special Flood Hazard Area** (mandatory flood insurance for mortgaged buyers)
3. **Alquist-Priolo Earthquake Fault Zone** (strict rebuild/addition setbacks)
4. **Seismic Hazard Zone** (liquefaction or landslide risk)
5. **Dam Inundation Zone**
6. **Mello-Roos Community Facilities District & 1915 Bond Assessments** (can add **$2,000 to $8,000+/year** on top of base 1% Proposition 13 property taxes)

### 5. Wood Destroying Pests and Organisms (WDO / Termite) Report: Section 1 vs. Section 2
In California, licensed structural pest inspectors split findings into two distinct categories:
- **Section 1 (Active Problems):** Active subterranean or drywood termites, wood-boring beetles, dry rot, or active fungus damage. Lenders and buyers traditionally treat Section 1 items as immediate repair or credit targets.
- **Section 2 (Conditions Likely to Lead to Infestation):** Earth-to-wood contact, leaky shower pans, clogged gutters, or faulty grade levels that haven't caused active rot yet—but will if left unaddressed.
- **Further Inspection Items:** Areas the inspector couldn't access (e.g., locked attics, heavy storage in garages, or tile showers not water-tested). Never ignore "Further Inspection Recommended."

## 4 Expensive Contradictions Buyers Miss Across Multi-File Packets

The biggest danger in a California disclosure packet isn't any single form—it's **contradictions across separate PDFs**:

1. **Bedrooms/Baths Mismatch (Unpermitted ADUs or Garage Conversions):** The MLS listing advertises *4 beds / 3 baths, 2,150 sq ft*, while the county tax record buried on page 118 shows *3 beds / 2 baths, 1,720 sq ft* with no finaled building permits attached.
2. **Recalled Electrical Panels Hidden in Inspection Photos:** The seller checks *"No known electrical defects"* on the TDS, while page 49 of the home inspection lists a **Zinsco**, **Sylvania**, or **Federal Pacific Stab-Lok** breaker panel ($3,500–$6,000 replacement, often required before insurers issue a policy). See our breakdown of [5 Seller Disclosure Red Flags Homebuyers Miss](/blog/5-disclosure-red-flags/).
3. **Sewer Lateral Offset & Root Intrusion:** Older California cities (especially in the East Bay, Los Angeles, and older Sacramento/San Diego neighborhoods) increasingly mandate **Point-of-Sale Sewer Lateral Compliance**, costing **$6,000 to $18,000** for trenchless replacement if the responsibility is shifted to the buyer in the addendum.
4. **HOA Underfunded Reserves (Condos & Townhomes):** If you are buying into an HOA, California's **Balcony Law (SB 326)** has triggered five-figure special assessments statewide. Read our guide on [Spotting Special Assessments in HOA Reserve Studies](/blog/hoa-reserve-study-special-assessment-red-flags/).

## Frequently Asked Questions About California Seller Disclosures

### Can a California seller skip the TDS by selling "As-Is"?
No. Unless the transaction falls under a narrow statutory exemption (such as probate, trust administration where the trustee never occupied the property, foreclosure/REO, or court-ordered transfers), "as-is" sales in California still require a completed TDS, NHD, and statutory disclosures of known material facts.

### How long do I have to cancel after receiving late disclosures in California?
Under California Civil Code § 1102.3, if a material disclosure (or amended TDS) is delivered *after* you execute an offer, you generally have **3 days** (if delivered in person) or **5 days** (if delivered electronically or by mail) to rescind your offer, even if you originally wrote a non-contingent offer.

### Should I ask for Section 1 repairs or a closing credit?
Most buyers prefer a **seller repair credit toward closing costs** (or price reduction) rather than asking the seller to perform repairs prior to close—so you can choose your own licensed contractor rather than relying on the cheapest patch job the seller can find.
