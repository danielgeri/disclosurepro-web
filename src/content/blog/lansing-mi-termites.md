## The Hidden Threat: Termite Vulnerabilities Across Lansing and Ingham County

Lansing’s river basin hydrology, dense glacial clay soils, and prevalent foundation types create substantial vulnerabilities to subterranean termite damage.

### Why River Basin Moisture and Concrete Block Foundations Accelerate Infestations

Several localized environmental and architectural dynamics amplify termite risks in Lansing neighborhoods:

- **Grand River Basin Hydrology and Clay Loam Subsoils:** Lansing is bisected by the Grand River and the Red Cedar River. The surrounding soils consist of dense glacial drift and clay loam with poor vertical percolation. Heavy spring snowmelts and rainfall saturate foundation perimeter soils. Subterranean termites require constant ambient humidity to avoid fatal dehydration; the damp subsoils beneath Lansing basements provide an uninterrupted ecological haven.
- **Hollow Concrete Block (CMU) Foundations:** Homes built from the 1920s through the 1970s across the Westside, Moores Park, and Colonial Village frequently sit on foundations constructed of concrete masonry units (cinder blocks). Eastern Subterranean Termites exploit the hollow interior cores of these blocks, ascending vertically through the center of the foundation wall directly into the untreated wood mudsill without building visible exploratory mud tubes on the interior basement face.
- **Basement Coal Chutes, Window Wells, and Earth Contacts:** Historic Lansing properties frequently retain abandoned cellar coal delivery chutes and ground-level basement windows that sit at or below external grade. When garden beds, landscaping mulch, or wood patio steps are built up against these openings over time, subterranean insects gain direct, concealed access to structural wood framing.
- **Heated Basement Microclimates:** Michigan winters bring freezing temperatures and a frost depth extending 36 to 42 inches. Subterranean termites survive by burrowing deep into subsoils. However, residential basements equipped with central boilers, hydronic heating pipes, and warm domestic water lines keep adjacent foundation soil warm enough for termite colonies to remain actively foraging inside wall cavities twelve months a year.
- **Neighborhood Architectural Vulnerabilities in Lansing:**
  - *Westside Historic Neighborhood:* 1910s–1930s Craftsman bungalows and American Foursquares featuring heavy oak framing, unvented rear utility crawlspaces, and low-clearance cellar spaces.
  - *Moores Park & Potter-Walsh:* Historic riverfront properties where high water tables and mature tree root systems drive moisture directly against foundation walls.
  - *Colonial Village & Churchill Downs:* 1950s brick ranches with finished basement family rooms where wood furring strips and drywall conceal perimeter foundation sill plates.

### Pest Biology: The Eastern Subterranean Termite in Mid-Michigan

Homeowners in Ingham County contend primarily with one prolific wood-destroying pest:

- **Eastern Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes flavipes*):** These social insects establish massive underground colonies containing hundreds of thousands to over a million individuals. Worker termites forage non-stop through moist soil and foundation voids for cellulose found in structural framing, subflooring, and paper-faced drywall. Because exposure to dry open air is lethal, they construct protective earthen mud shelter tubes composed of soil particles, saliva, and excrement across masonry surfaces. They feed along the softer spring growth rings of wood, hollowing out structural floor timbers while leaving the exterior surface deceptively intact.
- **Swarming Patterns in Lansing:** Winged reproductive alates swarm on warm, sunny spring mornings—typically following a gentle rain shower between April and late May. Sighting dozens or hundreds of shed translucent wings on basement windowsills, front vestibules, or porch steps is an unmistakable sign that a mature colony is nesting beneath or adjacent to the foundation.

## Michigan Real Estate Disclosure Laws: The Seller Disclosure Act (MCL 565.951)

Residential real estate transactions in Michigan are governed by statutory seller disclosure obligations established to protect prospective buyers from concealed structural defects.

### The Statutory Framework: MCL 565.951 et seq.

Under the Michigan Seller Disclosure Act (Public Act 92 of 1993, MCL 565.951 through MCL 565.966), sellers of residential property consisting of one to four units must deliver a completed Seller's Disclosure Statement to prospective buyers before executing a purchase agreement:

- **The Michigan Seller's Disclosure Statement (MCL 565.957):** The statutory disclosure form requires sellers to disclose their actual knowledge regarding property condition and pest history under **"Property conditions, improvements & additional information"**:
  - *"History of infestation, if any: (termites, carpenter ants, etc.)"*
  - *"Environmental problems: Are you aware of any other environmental problems?"*
  - *"Structural defects: Foundation, roof, structural framing, carrying beams, and subfloors."*
- **The Good Faith Standard:** Under MCL 565.960, each disclosure required by the Act must be made in *good faith*, defined as "honesty in fact in the conduct of the transaction." If a seller is aware of prior termite swarms, has paid for chemical barrier treatments, or has observed rotted floor framing, they are legally prohibited from concealing that information.
- **Common Law Fraud and Innocent Misrepresentation:** In addition to statutory remedies under the Seller Disclosure Act, Michigan recognizes claims for common law fraudulent concealment, silent fraud, and innocent misrepresentation. Under Michigan Supreme Court precedent, if a seller conceals a material latent defect that cannot be discovered upon reasonable buyer inspection, the seller remains liable for resulting financial damages.
- **The NPMA-33 Wood Destroying Insect Report:** In Michigan, certified structural pest inspections are documented on Form NPMA-33. The report catalogs active infestations, evidence of prior treatments, visible structural wood damage, and explicitly enumerates inaccessible areas of the home.

### Common Disclosure Evasions in Lansing Real Estate

Sellers and listing agents in Ingham County frequently use minimal or ambiguous language on the disclosure form to satisfy statutory requirements while minimizing the appearance of structural compromise:

- *"Perimeter chemical barrier applied around north foundation wall in 2021 by local pest control company as preventative maintenance."* (Conceals that the treatment was prompted by active mud tubes discovered in the basement workshop).
- *"Previous minor moisture rot repaired in basement sill plate near laundry area."* (Masks the fact that subterranean termites tunneled through damp timber surrounding an old drain pipe).
- *"Basement block foundation tuckpointed and sealed with waterproofing paint."* (Disguises the fact that hollow cinder blocks served as a concealed highway for termites into main floor joists).

- The statutory Michigan Seller's Disclosure Statement.
- A 40-to-60-page general home inspection report.
- The certified NPMA-33 Wood Destroying Insect report.

### The Inherent Limitation of General Home Inspections in Michigan

Many buyers assume a general home inspection will detect all insect damage. However, Michigan home inspectors adhere to national standards of practice (such as ASHI or InterNACHI) that explicitly exclude pest detection:

> *"The home inspector is not required to observe, identify, or report on wood-destroying organisms, termites, carpenter ants, or pests, nor are they required to dismantle finished basement walls, remove insulation, or enter low-clearance crawlspaces."*

In Lansing homes featuring finished basements with wood paneling, drywall, or suspended ceiling tiles, a general home inspector will not cut exploratory holes. If a homebuyer does not cross-examine the inaccessible areas cited on the NPMA-33 report against the seller’s responses on the Seller's Disclosure Statement, extensive termite destruction will stay concealed until floorboards begin to sag.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Lansing, MI

### 1. Are termite inspections required by law when purchasing a home in Lansing, Michigan?
Michigan state law does not legally mandate a pest inspection for cash sales. However, institutional mortgage lenders—including conventional, FHA, and VA loan programs—routinely require a clean NPMA-33 Wood Destroying Insect report before approving loan documents. In Ingham County’s older neighborhoods, where subterranean termites regularly invade hollow concrete block foundations, an independent WDI inspection is essential for every buyer.

### 2. Can subterranean termites survive freezing Mid-Michigan winters?
Yes. Subterranean termites survive cold Michigan winters by retreating deep into the soil below the frost line (which typically extends 36 to 42 inches in Lansing). Additionally, older residential basements equipped with central heating boilers, furnaces, or warm water pipes keep adjacent foundation soil warm enough for termites to remain active year-round inside structural wall cavities.

### 3. What legal remedy exists if a Lansing seller failed to disclose known termite damage?
Under the Michigan Seller Disclosure Act (MCL 565.951) and Michigan common law fraud doctrines (silent fraud and fraudulent misrepresentation), a buyer who proves that a seller knowingly concealed known termite activity or related structural damage can recover actual damages and court costs. To prevail in the Ingham County Circuit Court, the buyer must demonstrate that the seller possessed actual knowledge of the defect prior to closing and failed to disclose it in good faith.

### 4. What visible red flags indicate potential termite damage during a Lansing home tour?
Homebuyers should watch closely for:
- Earthen mud shelter tubes crawling up basement concrete block walls, chimney bases, or support posts.
- Piles of shed translucent wings along basement window wells, cellar steps, or baseboards in the spring.
- Spongy, squishy, or noticeably sloping floorboards, especially near exterior perimeter walls or below bathrooms.
- Distinct pinholes in drywall or wood baseboards where paper facing appears sunken or blistered.
