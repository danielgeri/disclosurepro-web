## WDO vs. WDI Termite Inspections: What Is the Difference?

Real estate contracts, mortgage lenders, and state agriculture boards use two similar acronyms that do **not** cover the exact same defects:

- **WDI Report (Wood-Destroying Insect Report):** Focuses exclusively on **insects** that feed on or nest inside structural wood—specifically subterranean termites, Formosan termites, drywood termites, dampwood termites, carpenter ants, carpenter bees, and wood-boring beetles (powderpost beetles, old house borers). Used across most of the U.S. via the **NPMA-33** form, including Texas, Virginia, North Carolina, Massachusetts, Utah, and Ohio.
- **WDO Report (Wood-Destroying Organism Report):** Covers every wood-destroying insect listed above **PLUS wood-decaying fungi (brown rot, white rot, and dry rot)** caused by chronic moisture intrusion. Required in high-moisture or high-regulation states such as **California (SPCB Branch 3)** and **Florida (FDACS-13645)**.

### What a Licensed Termite Inspector Actually Checks

A state-licensed structural pest inspector conducts a **visual and sounding (probe) evaluation** of all *accessible* framing and foundation areas, including:
- Foundation perimeters, crawlspace piers, stem walls, and expansion joints for subterranean **mud shelter tubes**.
- Attic trusses, exposed eaves, window frames, garage rafters, and fascia boards for drywood **frass pellets** and kick-out pinholes.
- Mudsills, band joists, porch framing, and under-bathroom subfloors for hollow-sounding wood, soft probe spots, and active fungal decay.
- Prior drill plugs, bait-station remnants, or treatment stickers placed near electrical panels or sink cabinets.
- **Conducive conditions**—such as earth-to-wood contact, stucco extending below soil grade, leaking shower pans, or firewood stacked against siding.

## Official State Termite Report Forms Compared (NPMA-33, CA, FL, TX & More)

Because termite regulation is enforced at the state level, the exact form you receive—and what triggers mandatory seller clearance—depends on where you are buying:

| State / Scope | Official Form Name | Key Report Sections & Buyer Traps |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nationwide / HUD / VA / FHA** | **NPMA-33** (*Wood Destroying Insect Inspection Report*) | Standard federal lending form. Watch **Section II (Visible Evidence)**, **Section III (Previous Treatment)**, and especially **Section IV (Obstructed & Inaccessible Areas)**. |
| **California** | **SPCB Branch 3 WDO Report** (*Bus. & Prof. Code § 8516*) | Splits findings into **Section 1** (active infestation/rot & existing damage), **Section 2** (conducive conditions), and **Further Inspection** items. | **Florida** | **FDACS-13645** (*Wood-Destroying Organisms Inspection Report*) | Tracks subterranean, Formosan & drywood termites plus wood-decaying fungi; requires posting a **Notice of Inspection sticker** (usually inside the electric panel or under the kitchen sink). |
| **Texas** | **TDA SPCS Official WDI Report** (*Texas NPMA-33*) | Must be issued by a TDA-licensed pest technician (separate from TREC home inspectors). Includes mandatory **Section 7 Conducive Conditions** and a **Section 10 Graph Diagram**. See our [Dallas, TX WDI guide](/blog/dallas-tx-termites/). |
| **Southeast & Hawaii** | **NC WDIR**, **Official Georgia WIR ("Termite Letter")**, **Hawaii TIR** | Often tied directly to closing contingencies (e.g., Hawaii HAR Paragraph L-2 covers eradication treatment, **not** automatic structural wood repair unless separately negotiated). |

## Five Red Flags Buried Inside WDO & WDI Reports That Buyers Miss

Even a report that looks "clean" on Page 1 frequently hides expensive liabilities in small checkboxes and diagram footnotes:

### 1. The "Obstructed or Inaccessible Areas" Exemption
Every official WDO/WDI report is strictly limited to **visible, accessible areas**. Inspectors cannot tear down finished basement drywall, lift glued hardwood flooring, crawl beneath 12-inch pier-and-beam joists, peel back closed-cell spray-foam insulation, or move stacks of seller storage boxes. If 40% to 70% of the perimeter foundation or vaulted ceiling is checked "Inaccessible," a clean report is **not a guarantee of a termite-free home**.

### 2. "Evidence of Previous Treatment — No Live Insects Observed"
Seeing "no live activity" lulls buyers into waiving contingencies. Remember:
- A pest inspector can only see what is active on the surface *that day*.
- Previous liquid soil barriers typically degrade after **5 to 10 years**, and older drywood spot treatments never protect adjacent rafters.
- Most importantly, chemical treatment kills insects—**it never restores structural strength to hollowed sills, joists, or headers**. Always follow the checklist in our [buying a house with a history of termites disclosure guide](/blog/buying-house-with-termite-history-disclosure-guide/).

### 3. Conducive Conditions (Section 2 / Section 7 Warnings)
Inspectors routinely note earth-to-wood deck posts, concrete form boards left in crawlspaces, faulty stucco weep screeds, clogged gutters, or leaking shower pans. Even when no live termites exist today, conducive conditions are an open invitation for colonies—and many **transferable termite bonds are voided** if conducive conditions remain uncorrected.

### 4. Seller "Clearance Shopping" (Spot Foam vs. Whole-House Tenting)
When sellers order their own pre-listing WDO report and receive a $4,500 whole-house Vikane tent fumigation bid, some shop around for a second pest company willing to perform a $750 localized spot-foam injection just to issue a "Section 1 Clearance." Always ask whether any earlier WDO/WDI reports were completed within the last 12–48 months.

### 5. Cross-PDF Contradictions With Seller Disclosures & General Inspections
The most dangerous defects rarely appear in a single PDF. For example, a WDI inspector may check *"No visible termites; south wall obstructed by paneling,"* while the general home inspector notes *"25% wood moisture and soft baseboards on south wall"* on Page 54—and the seller's disclosure statement mentions *"replaced two warped floorboards in 2023."*

## VA, FHA & Conventional Lender Rules: When Is a Termite Report Mandatory?

Even when state law does not mandate a termite inspection, your mortgage lender can stall or halt closing if WDI/WDO conditions are triggered:

- **VA Loans:** Under VA underwriting guidelines tied to the **HUD/VA Termite Infestation Probability Map**, properties located in "very heavy" or "moderate-to-heavy" termite zones require a clean **NPMA-33 (or state-equivalent WDO/WDI) report** before funding. While updated VA rules allow buyers to pay the WDI inspection fee where permitted by contract, **active infestation treatment and structural safety repairs** must be cleared before closing (typically negotiated to the seller).
- **FHA Loans:** FHA does not automatically require a termite inspection on every existing home unless the property is in a high-risk area, the FHA appraiser observes visible evidence of active infestation or wood rot, local custom mandates it, or the purchase contract requires one. Once an appraiser flags possible WDO damage, a licensed clearance becomes mandatory.
- **Conventional Loans:** Conventional lenders rarely demand a WDI report upfront—**unless** the purchase contract references one or an appraiser/inspector report sent to underwriting mentions active termite damage or structural wood decay.

### How to Negotiate Repair Credits After a Bad WDO / WDI Report

1. **Separate Eradication From Structural Carpentry:** Never accept a pest company's $1,800 chemical treatment quote as the full cost of the problem. Require a **licensed general contractor or structural engineer** to bid compromised sills, joists, headers, and permit fees separately.
2. **Watch Lender Concession Caps:** If you negotiate a closing cost credit rather than a price reduction, ensure total seller credits stay within your loan program's Interested Party Contribution (IPC) cap (typically 3%–9% on Conventional, 6% on FHA, 4% on VA).
3. **Avoid Pre-Closing Seller Patchwork:** Unless your lender strictly mandates pre-close eradication clearance (such as VA/FHA active-infestation clearance), request a **closing credit or escrow holdback** for structural carpentry so you control the contractor, materials, and permits after closing.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Termite Inspections (WDO & WDI Reports)

### What is a WDO or WDI termite inspection and how much does it cost?
A WDO (Wood-Destroying Organism) or WDI (Wood-Destroying Insect) inspection is a specialized visual and sounding inspection performed by a state-licensed pest control inspector—usually costing **$75 to $250**—that checks accessible foundation walls, crawlspaces, attics, and framing for active termites, prior damage, previous treatments, and conducive conditions.

### Does a standard home inspection include a termite inspection?
Usually no. General home inspectors look for major system and structural defects, and standard inspection contracts exclude formal WDI/WDO pest certifications unless the inspector holds a separate state structural pest control license and you specifically add a WDO/WDI inspection to your order.

### Who pays for a termite inspection and termite repairs when buying a house?
In most U.S. markets, the **buyer** pays the $75–$250 inspection fee during the due-diligence contingency period (though sellers sometimes pay in California or Hawaii depending on local contract custom). If active termites or wood damage are discovered, **who pays for chemical treatment and structural repairs is negotiated** via a repair/credit addendum—with sellers frequently covering active eradication to satisfy lender clearance rules.

### What happens if a termite inspection finds damage or active termites before closing?
You typically have three options before your inspection contingency expires: (1) demand the seller pay a licensed pest operator for full eradication and provide a clean clearance certificate before closing; (2) negotiate a **closing cost credit or price reduction** backed by contractor bids to cover structural framing repairs; or (3) exercise your inspection contingency to walk away with your earnest money intact if the damage exceeds your risk tolerance or the seller refuses to negotiate.
