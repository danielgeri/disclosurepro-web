## The Hidden Threat: Dual Termite Infestations Across Gresham, OR

Gresham’s heavy Pacific Northwest rainfall, volcanic loam soils, and extensive crawlspace construction combine to produce severe structural vulnerabilities to wood-destroying organisms.

### Why Pacific Northwest Rain and Crawlspaces Accelerate Infestations

Several localized environmental and architectural dynamics make Gresham properties vulnerable to wood-destroying insect colonization:

- **Heavy Annual Precipitation and Persistent Soil Dampness:** Gresham receives over 45 inches of rainfall annually, with precipitation concentrated heavily between October and May. Chronic rainfall saturates the region's rich volcanic and alluvial soils. Subterranean termites require continuous ambient moisture to survive; the perpetually damp subsoils of East Multnomah County provide an optimal foraging environment for large underground colonies.
- **Raised Foundations and Unventilated Crawlspaces:** The vast majority of homes in Kelly Creek, Powell Valley, and Holly Brook built between 1960 and 1995 sit atop raised perimeter concrete foundations with post-and-beam crawlspaces. Many vintage crawlspaces feature inadequate cross-ventilation, missing or torn polyethylene vapor barriers, and chronic standing water from high winter water tables. Subfloor Douglas fir timbers—including mudsills, pony wall studs, and floor joists—absorb moisture from the soil, creating a prime food source for wood-destroying pests.
- **Exterior Water Intrusion and Dampwood Termite Habitats:** Frequent rainfall, combined with moss buildup on roofs and clogged gutters overflowing against foundation footings, leads to chronic exterior wood decay. While subterranean termites invade from the ground, Pacific Dampwood Termites (*Zootermopsis angusticollis*) attack exterior architectural features directly, colonizing leaking deck ledger boards, window trim, and unsealed siding without needing ground contact.
- **Neighborhood Architectural Vulnerabilities in Gresham:**
  - *Historic Downtown Gresham:* 1910s-1930s Craftsman bungalows featuring rough-sawn dimensional lumber, exterior wood siding, and direct earth-to-wood contact along covered front porches where termites enter undetected.
  - *Kelly Creek & Powell Valley:* 1960s-1970s daylight ranches and split-levels built on sloping terrain where rear foundation framing sits close to moist garden beds and bark mulch.
  - *Pleasant Valley & Holly Brook:* 1990s-2000s two-story suburban homes where rear cedar decks and patio covers attached without proper flashing allow moisture and dampwood termites to penetrate the home's rim joists.

### Pest Biology: Subterranean and Dampwood Species in East Multnomah County

Homebuyers in Gresham must understand the differences between the two primary wood-destroying species active in the region:

- **Western Subterranean Termite (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** These social insects establish extensive underground colonies containing hundreds of thousands of workers. Lacking an outer cuticle that retains moisture, they must remain in damp soil or construct airtight earthen mud shelter tubes to navigate concrete foundation footings. They consume structural wood from the inside out along the softer spring growth rings, leaving an outer shell of wood or paint deceptively intact while devastating internal load-bearing strength.
- **Pacific Dampwood Termite (*Zootermopsis angusticollis*):** The largest termite species in North America, winged swarmers can measure up to an inch in length. Unlike subterranean termites, dampwood termites do not build mud tubes and do not require soil contact. Instead, they colonize structural wood that has already absorbed moisture from plumbing leaks, roof runoff, or chronic crawlspace dampness. They hollow out cavernous galleries across wood grain, plugging external entry holes with a distinctive paste of chewed wood and fecal pellets.
- **Swarming Periods in East Multnomah County:** Western Subterranean Termites swarm on warm, sunny afternoons following spring rains (April through June). Pacific Dampwood Termites swarm on warm, humid late-summer and early-autumn evenings (August through October), frequently flying in large numbers toward porch lights and illuminated windows. Observing swarms of large winged insects or finding piles of shed wings around crawlspace access hatches is conclusive evidence of an active infestation.

## Oregon Real Estate Disclosure Laws: ORS 105.462 to 105.490

Oregon maintains a strict, statutory disclosure framework designed to protect residential buyers, backed by a powerful statutory right of contract revocation.

### The Statutory Framework: ORS 105.464

Under Oregon Revised Statutes (ORS 105.462 to 105.490), a seller of residential real property must deliver a completed, written Oregon Seller’s Property Disclosure Statement to each prospective buyer who makes a written offer:

- **The Mandatory Statutory Disclosure Form (ORS 105.464):** The Oregon disclosure statement contains comprehensive statutory questions addressing structural condition and pest infestation:
  - *Section 4 (Structural / Pest):* Specifically asks: *"Are there any pest infestations or wood-destroying organism damage?"* and *"Has the property been treated for termites, wood-destroying insects, or dry rot? If yes, provide dates, company, and copies of reports."*
  - *Section 4 (Foundation & Drainage):* Requires disclosure of any standing water, drainage problems, or settling affecting the foundation or crawlspace.
- **The Five-Business-Day Statutory Right of Revocation (ORS 105.475):** Oregon law provides buyers with an extraordinary safeguard: If the seller delivers the disclosure statement, the buyer has five business days from receipt to revoke their offer and demand the immediate return of their earnest money deposit for any reason or no reason at all. If the seller fails to deliver the statutory disclosure statement before closing, the buyer may revoke the transaction at any time prior to closing.
- **The Knowledge Standard and Fraud Claims:** Under ORS 105.464, the seller’s answers are based on the seller’s actual knowledge at the time of disclosure. However, Oregon courts (*Caldwell v. Pop’s Homes*, *Bodenhamer v. Patterson*) make clear that a seller who actively conceals known structural damage, makes fraudulent misrepresentations, or answers "Unknown" when they have actual knowledge can be held liable in Multnomah County Circuit Court for actual damages, fraud, and attorney's fees.
- **Form NPMA-33 Wood Destroying Insect Report:** For buyers obtaining financing through FHA, VA, or conventional mortgage programs, lenders frequently require a certified NPMA-33 Wood Destroying Insect report completed by an inspector licensed by the Oregon Department of Agriculture (ODA).

### Common Disclosure Evasions in Gresham Real Estate

In East Multnomah County transactions, sellers often craft vague disclosure entries to minimize the appearance of pest problems:

- *"Perimeter crawlspace barrier treatment performed in 2021 by local exterminator as preventative pest maintenance."* (Masks the fact that subterranean mud tubes were discovered on crawlspace post pads).
- *"Crawlspace vapor barrier replaced and localized wood shoring installed in 2023."* (Disguises that dampwood termites and wood rot had severely hollowed out main carrying beams).
- *"Exterior deck flashed and recaulked during exterior painting refresh."* (Conceals that water intrusion behind the deck ledger board had initiated deep dampwood termite galleries into the home's rim joists).

### The Inherent Limitation of General Home Inspections in Oregon

Many homebuyers mistakenly believe that a standard home inspection covers all wood-destroying insect liabilities. However, home inspectors licensed by the Oregon Construction Contractors Board (CCB) operate under strict administrative rules (OAR 812-008-0200 et seq.) that define the inspection scope:

> *"The general home inspector is not required to observe, identify, or report on wood-destroying organisms, termites, powder-post beetles, or fungal decay, nor is the inspector required to dismantle finished building components, move insulation, or enter crawlspaces with standing water, inadequate clearance, or hazardous obstructions."*

In Gresham homes where low-clearance crawlspaces, ductwork, or perimeter insulation obstruct visual access, general inspectors frequently flag these areas as "inaccessible." If a buyer fails to compare the pest inspector's list of inaccessible crawlspace zones against the seller's disclosure answers, extensive subterranean and dampwood termite damage can remain completely hidden until floor joists sag or support posts buckle.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Gresham, OR

### 1. Are termite inspections mandatory when buying a home in Gresham, OR?
Oregon law does not legally mandate a termite inspection for cash real estate sales. However, institutional mortgage lenders underwriting VA, FHA, and many conventional loans routinely require a certified NPMA-33 Wood Destroying Insect inspection report prior to loan approval. In Gresham, where high rainfall and crawlspace moisture foster both subterranean and dampwood termites, an independent inspection is an indispensable safeguard.

### 2. What is the difference between subterranean and dampwood termites in Oregon?
Western Subterranean Termites nest underground in the soil and build earthen mud tubes to reach structural wood, consuming wood from the inside out. Pacific Dampwood Termites are much larger, do not live in the soil, and do not construct mud tubes. Instead, dampwood termites establish colonies directly inside wood that has absorbed moisture from rainfall, roof leaks, or wet crawlspaces.

### 3. What is Oregon's 5-day right of revocation regarding seller disclosures?
Under ORS 105.475, a buyer who receives the statutory Oregon Seller's Property Disclosure Statement has five business days to review it. During this five-day window, the buyer has the absolute right to revoke their purchase offer and receive a full refund of their earnest money deposit for any reason or no reason at all.

### 4. What visible warning signs should I watch for during a Gresham home tour?
Key indicators include:
- Earthen mud shelter tubes crawling up concrete foundation footings or support piers in the crawlspace.
- Discarded translucent insect wings clustered around crawlspace vents, windowsills, or exterior porch lights in spring or late summer.
- Soft, spongy floorboards, particularly near exterior doors, crawlspace access hatches, or sliding patio doors.
- Patched circular drill holes spaced 12 inches apart along the crawlspace foundation footing or garage floor, indicating past pressurized chemical treatments.
