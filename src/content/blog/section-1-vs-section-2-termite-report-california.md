Nearly every California home purchase packet includes a **Wood Destroying Pests and Organisms (WDO) Inspection Report**—commonly called a "termite report"—regulated by the **California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB)**. Yet first-time and repeat buyers alike routinely misread the cost table near the back of the report, either panicking over routine preventive recommendations or overlooking hidden structural dry rot that carries a **$15,000+ repair price tag**.

Understanding the legal and financial difference between **Section 1**, **Section 2**, and **Further Inspection** findings—and how they interact with the rest of your [California TDS and SPQ disclosure packet](/blog/california-tds-spq-disclosure-guide/)—gives you immediate leverage before removing contingencies.

---

## Why California Splits WDO Findings Into Separated Sections

Under **California Business and Professions Code § 8516** and **16 CCR § 1990**, whenever a buyer, seller, or mortgage lender requests a "separated report," a Branch 3 licensed structural pest inspector must categorize every observation into three distinct buckets:

### 1. Section 1: Active Infestations and Existing Damage
**Section 1** covers conditions where wood-destroying pests or organisms are **actively present right now**, or where past infestations/moisture have already caused structural damage that requires repair or treatment to obtain a clean **Notice of Work Completed and Not Completed ("Termite Clearance")**:

- **Active Subterranean Termites:** Mud shelter tubes climbing foundation stem walls, piers, or garage framing—typically treated via soil trenching and termiticide injection (**$1,200 – $3,200**).
- **Active Drywood Termites:** Six-sided fecal pellets (*frass*) or kick-out holes in attic rafters, fascia boards, or window sills. Whole-structure tent fumigation with Vikane gas typically runs **$2,200 – $5,500+** depending on cubic footage, whereas localized "spot treatments" (borates, orange oil, or microwave) carry higher recurrence risk.
- **Fungus & Dry Rot Damage:** Wood-decaying fungi in fascia boards, rafter tails, exterior window trim, deck ledgers, bathroom subfloors around leaking wax rings, or shower pans (**$1,800 – $18,000+** when framing or tiled shower pans must be demolished and rebuilt).
- **Wood-Boring Beetles:** Powderpost or deathwatch beetle exit holes in crawlspace joists or hardwood subflooring.

### 2. Section 2: Conditions Likely to Lead to Future Infestation or Infection
**Section 2** flags building or site defects that have **not yet caused active rot or termite activity**, but create the exact moisture or access pathway that will cause Section 1 damage if left uncorrected:

- **Earth-to-Wood Contact:** Soil, mulch, or planter beds touching wood siding, fence posts bolted directly to the house, or untreated porch framing.
- **Faulty Grade Levels:** Exterior soil sitting higher than the top of the concrete foundation stem wall, allowing rainwater to wick behind stucco weep screeds.
- **Plumbing Drips & Missing Grout/Caulking:** Cracked tile grout in a primary shower, dripping P-traps under sinks, or missing roof flashing kick-outs.
- **Clogged Gutters or Missing Downspout Extensions:** Dumping roof runoff directly against foundation footings.

### 3. "Further Inspection" (Inaccessible Areas) — The Quietest Financial Trap
Inspectors are only permitted to examine visible, accessible areas without moving heavy personal belongings, prying open finished drywall, or testing second-story showers over finished ceilings unless authorized. Whenever an inspector writes **"Inaccessible — Further Inspection Recommended"**, buyers assume "no news is good news."

In reality, **Further Inspection** items frequently conceal the costliest defects in the entire transaction:
- Locked attic hatches or low-clearance crawlspaces blocked by HVAC ducts
- Heavy seller storage stacked wall-to-wall inside a garage
- Raised wood decks built inches above dirt with no crawl access
- Stalls where the inspector saw subfloor moisture staining from below and recommended a **24-hour shower pan water test**

## Who Pays for Section 1 Work in California? (Myth vs. Modern Contract Law)

Ask someone who bought a California home 20 years ago and they will tell you: *"The seller always has to pay for Section 1, and the buyer pays for Section 2."*

**That is no longer automatic under California's Purchase Agreement.** Over a decade ago, the California Association of Realtors (C.A.R.) removed the pre-printed Wood Destroying Pest Allocation addendum from the standard Residential Purchase Agreement (RPA). Today:

1. **Everything Is Negotiated by Contract:** Unless your offer explicitly requests Section 1 clearance—or you negotiate a [seller repair credit addendum after reviewing inspections](/blog/how-to-write-repair-credit-addendum-after-inspection/)—the seller has **zero automatic contractual obligation** to complete or pay for Section 1 items.
2. **Pre-Listing Reports in Competitive Markets:** In Bay Area, Los Angeles, and coastal San Diego bidding wars, listing agents routinely order a pre-listing WDO report, upload it to the disclosure portal alongside the TDS and general home inspection, and expect buyers to price those Section 1 costs into their initial offer.
3. **VA, FHA & Conventional Lender Rules:**
   - **VA Loans:** Generally require a pest inspection in California and mandate completion of **Section 1** repairs (clean WDO certification) prior to loan funding, because wood-destroying damage threatens Minimum Property Requirements (MPRs).
   - **FHA Loans:** Require a WDO clearance if the appraiser observes active infestation, dry rot, or earth-to-wood hazards during the FHA appraisal.
   - **Conventional Loans:** Rarely ask to see the WDO report *unless* you inadvertently reference "termite and dry rot structural damage" directly inside a purchase contract amendment sent to the mortgage underwriter! (That is why experienced agents phrase closing credits simply as a *"Seller credit toward Buyer's recurring and non-recurring closing costs"* rather than listing structural defects on the contract addendum.)

## Real-World Case Study: The $4,100 Report That Turned Into $19,400

Consider a buyer purchasing a 1962 ranch home in Orange County. Attached to the 140-page disclosure packet was a pre-listing WDO report quoting **$4,150** for Section 1 items:
- Item 3A: Spot treatment for drywood termites in garage rafters ($850)
- Item 4A: Replace rotted fascia boards along rear patio eaves ($1,900)
- Item 6A: Repair toilet subfloor damage in hall bath ($1,400)

However, buried on page 7 under Item 6B was a two-line caveat: *"Damage extends into inaccessible wall framing behind tiled tub surround; supplemental report and estimate will be issued once tile and drywall are opened during repair."* Meanwhile, on the Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ), the seller had written *"minor slow drain in hall bath, snaked 2024."*

Had the buyer simply asked for a $4,150 credit based on the summary box, they would have absorbed **$15,250 in supplemental framing, mold remediation, and full tub/tile reconstruction** once the wall was opened after closing. Cross-referencing WDO "supplemental damage" disclaimers against seller water notes—one of the classic [5 seller disclosure red flags buyers miss](/blog/5-disclosure-red-flags/)—allowed the buyer to bring in a general contractor during the inspection contingency and secure a **$14,000 closing cost credit**.

## Condos & Townhomes: Who Owns Exterior Section 1 Repairs?

If you are purchasing a condo or townhome in an HOA, exterior siding, roof fascia, balconies, and subterranean foundations are usually **Common Area** maintained by the association—meaning an individual unit seller isn't even allowed to tent the building or replace exterior trim on their own.

However, do not celebrate prematurely:
- Check the [HOA Reserve Study and board meeting minutes](/blog/hoa-reserve-study-special-assessment-red-flags/) to see when the HOA last fumigated or repaired exterior wood rot, and whether **California SB 326 balcony inspections** uncovered widespread dry rot across the community that will trigger a special assessment right after you close.
- You are still typically responsible for interior unit fixtures (such as leaking toilet wax rings and shower pans).

## Frequently Asked Questions About California Section 1 & Section 2 Reports

### Can I look up past termite reports filed on a California house?
Yes. Every licensed California WDO inspection and Notice of Work Completed within the past **two to three years** must be filed with the **California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB)** database. You can search a property address free on the SPCB public WDO search portal—a great way to catch whether a seller ordered *two* inspections and only included the cheaper company's report in the disclosure packet!

### Should I ask the seller to tent the house before closing or give me a credit?
If your lender (e.g., Conventional) allows a closing credit, taking a **credit and scheduling tent fumigation yourself immediately after closing** (before moving furniture, food, and pets inside) is often cleaner—you choose a reputable fumigator with a multi-year warranty in your name, and you supervise roof-tile walk-damage repairs directly.

### Why does a WDO report say "Bid on Request" instead of a dollar price?
Pest control companies employ carpenters for basic fascia and subfloor repairs, not licensed roofing, tile, or structural foundation contractors. Whenever an item says **"Refer to appropriate licensed tradesperson"** or **"Bid on Request,"** the total repair figure at the bottom of the pest report **excludes** that item completely! Always add independent contractor estimates on top of the quoted Section 1 subtotal.
