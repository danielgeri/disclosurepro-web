## The Engineering of Convective Storm and Thermal Degradation in Arlington

Evaluating a roof in Arlington requires an understanding of how violent North Texas weather systems interact with modern roofing assemblies.

### Supercell Hail Storms: Granule Loss vs. Internal Mat Rupture
The DFW Metroplex experiences some of the costliest hailstorms in United States history:
- **Terminal Velocity and Kinetic Energy:** Dense hailstones ranging from 1.5 inches (walnut) to 3 inches (baseball) strike roofs at speeds exceeding 75 mph.
- **The Internal Mat Fracture:** While a buyer might notice only minor circular indentations on shingles (often dismissed by sellers as harmless "spatter"), high-velocity hail crushes the internal woven fiberglass substrate beneath the surface.
- **Progressive Solar Erosion:** Once the fiberglass reinforcement breaks, the asphalt coating is directly exposed to North Texas's brutal ultraviolet rays. Over the next 12 to 24 months, thermal cycling causes the fractured asphalt to crack and erode, allowing rainwater to penetrate through to the roof deck.

### Microburst Straight-Line Winds and Sealant Delamination
Severe spring thunderstorms in Tarrant County frequently produce powerful microburst downdrafts:
- **Torn Adhesive Strips:** Architectural shingles rely on a heat-activated asphalt sealant bead to bond adjacent overlapping tabs. Sustained 70+ mph winds peel these shingles upward, breaking the adhesive seal.
- **The "Invisible Hinge":** When unsealed tabs flap violently during thunderstorms, they crease along the top boundary of the shingle. Even if the shingles lay back down flat after the storm, the internal fiberglass is permanently cracked, allowing wind-driven rain to bypass the shingle during the next downpour.

### Triple-Digit Heatwaves and Thermal Baking
Arlington summers regularly bring 30 to 50 days of 100°F+ temperatures:
- **Rooftop Baking:** Surface temperatures on dark asphalt shingles easily reach 160°F to 175°F. This intense radiant heat boils away the volatile plasticizers and oils that keep asphalt pliable.
- **Brittle Cracking:** Dried-out shingles become rigid and brittle. When winter cold snaps plunge temperatures below freezing, the shingles cannot expand or contract, resulting in widespread stress fractures around nail heads.

## Texas Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Property Code § 5.008 and the DTPA Decoded

Texas has strict statutory disclosure mandates designed to inform buyers, but evasive answers and undisclosed storm insurance claims remain widespread.

### Texas Property Code § 5.008: The Seller's Disclosure Notice
Under Texas Property Code § 5.008, a seller of a single-family residential property must provide the buyer with a written **Seller's Disclosure Notice**:
- **Section 4 – Structural Conditions:** The seller must disclose the roof type, approximate roof age, and state whether they are aware of any defects or malfunctions in the roof.
- **Section 5 – Water Penetration and Claims:** Inquires directly whether there is any history of roof leaks, whether repairs have been made, whether any property insurance claims for hail or wind damage were filed, and whether insurance proceeds were received.
- **TREC Form OP-H vs. Texas REALTORS® Form TXR 1406:** While TREC publishes a statutory minimum form, Texas REALTORS® uses Form TXR 1406, which contains significantly more detailed questions regarding previous insurance claims and unpermitted repairs.

### The "Pocketed Insurance Claim" Epidemic in DFW
One of the most frequent forms of disclosure fraud in the DFW Metroplex revolves around storm insurance payouts:
- Following a major North Texas hailstorm, the seller files an insurance claim. The insurance company inspects the roof, confirms a total hail loss, and issues a settlement check for $18,000 to $25,000.
- Instead of replacing the roof, the seller hires an unlicensed handyman to replace a few cracked ridge shingles and apply black roofing tar around vents for $800, pocketing the remaining $20,000.
- The seller then lists the home, checking "No active leaks" on the disclosure notice. When the buyer purchases the property, their insurance carrier pulls the CLUE (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) database, discovers the unaddressed total loss claim, and immediately refuses to insure the roof or demands a mandatory full replacement at the buyer's expense.

### The Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA)
If a seller knowingly misrepresents the condition of a roof or conceals known storm damage, they can be sued under the **Texas Deceptive Trade Practices-Consumer Protection Act (DTPA)** (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 17.41 et seq.):
- **Treble Damages:** Under the DTPA, if a court finds that the seller acted "knowingly" or "intentionally," the buyer may be awarded up to **three times their actual economic damages**, plus court costs and reasonable attorney fees.
- **The "As-Is" Defense:** While standard Texas contracts (TREC One to Four Family Residential Contract) feature an "As-Is" clause in Paragraph 7D, the Texas Supreme Court held in *Prudential Ins. Co. of America v. Jefferson Associates, Ltd.* that an "As-Is" agreement is **unenforceable if induced by fraudulent representation or fraudulent concealment**.

### Evasive Phrasing in Arlington Seller Disclosures
When reviewing a Texas Seller's Disclosure Notice in Tarrant County, look out for these warning phrases:
- *"Roof inspected by roofer after spring hail; minor repairs completed"* — Frequently masks the reality that the roofer declared a total loss that the homeowner chose not to replace.
- *"Shingles checked annually; no active leaks"* — Often used to downplay brittle, 18-year-old shingles that are past their useful life and uninsurable.
- *"Insurance claim filed in 2023; claim closed"* — Fails to state whether the roof was fully replaced or if the insurance check was pocketed.
- *"Seller is an investor/estate; seller has never lived in the property"* — While common, it signals that the buyer must conduct an exhaustive, independent professional inspection.

## The Manual Review Problem: Navigating the Texas Option Period

In Texas residential real estate contracts, buyers negotiate an **Option Period** (typically 5 to 10 days) under Paragraph 23 of the TREC contract, for which they pay an independent Option Fee.

During this compressed option period, an Arlington buyer must coordinate:
1. The multi-page TREC or Texas REALTORS® Seller's Disclosure Notice.
2. The 50-to-70-page TREC standard inspection report.
3. City of Arlington Community Development & Planning portal permit records to confirm whether past reroofing permits were pulled and inspected.
4. Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange (CLUE) reports to uncover previous insurance storm payouts.
5. Insurance underwriting quotes to verify whether the carrier requires a mandatory 1% or 2% wind/hail deductible.

General home inspectors licensed by TREC are legally permitted to disclaim walking on roofs if deemed unsafe. Reports commonly note: *"Roof inspected from eaves via ladder and drone photography due to pitch and roof surface conditions."* Drones and ground photos cannot detect soft hail-bruised spots or lifted adhesive strips.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in Arlington

### 1. What are Class 4 impact-resistant shingles and why are they recommended in Arlington?
Class 4 impact-resistant shingles have passed the UL 2218 steel ball drop test, proving they can withstand 2-inch steel ball impacts without cracking the interior fiberglass mat. In Arlington’s severe hail climate, Class 4 shingles prevent premature storm failure, protect home insurability, and frequently earn homeowners annual insurance premium discounts of up to 20% to 28%.

### 2. How do percentage-based wind/hail deductibles work in Texas?
In Texas, most homeowner insurance policies mandate separate wind and hail deductibles calculated as a percentage of the home's total insured dwelling value (Coverage A), usually **1% or 2%**. On a $500,000 home, a 2% deductible means the buyer must pay the first **$10,000 out-of-pocket** before insurance covers any portion of a storm claim.

### 3. Can a Texas seller be sued under the DTPA for hiding roof leaks?
Yes. Under the Texas Deceptive Trade Practices Act (Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 17.41 et seq.), a seller who knowingly fails to disclose known roof defects or intentionally misrepresents the condition of a roof can be sued by the buyer. If the seller acted knowingly or intentionally, the buyer may recover up to three times their actual economic damages, plus attorney fees.

### 4. What is a TREC Option Period and how should an Arlington buyer use it?
The Option Period (Paragraph 23 of the TREC contract) grants the buyer the unrestricted right to terminate the contract within a negotiated number of days (usually 5 to 10 days) for a small option fee. An Arlington buyer should use this period to hire a dedicated roofing contractor to physically walk the roof, pull City of Arlington permit records, and order a CLUE report to verify past insurance claim history.
