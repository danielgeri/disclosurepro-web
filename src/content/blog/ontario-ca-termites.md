## The Hidden Threat: Termites in Ontario and the Inland Empire

Ontario is situated in the Pomona Valley region of the Inland Empire at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains. Our local climate features hot, arid summers, mild winters, intense autumn Santa Ana winds, and historically cultivated alluvial soils—conditions that support aggressive termite populations.

### Why Ontario Housing Stock and Regional Climate Elevate Pest Risk

Several architectural, geological, and climate factors make residential properties across Ontario particularly vulnerable to wood-destroying pest infestations:

- **Dual Species Infiltration (Subterranean & Drywood):** Unlike colder regions that face only subterranean threats, Ontario properties contend with simultaneous pressure from subterranean colonies in the soil and airborne drywood colonies entering attics, eaves, and exposed rafters during late-summer heatwaves.
- **Agricultural Heritage and Alluvial Soils:** Ontario was founded as a planned model citrus agricultural community. Decades of agricultural irrigation left nutrient-rich, deep alluvial soils that retain pockets of subterranean moisture, creating expansive underground networks for subterranean termite colonies.
- **Santa Ana Winds and Swarming Dispersion:** High-velocity, dry Santa Ana winds in late summer and autumn carry winged drywood termite swarmers (alates) for miles across San Bernardino County, depositing thousands of reproductive pests directly onto roof tiles, exposed rafter tails, and wooden fascia boards.
- **Architectural Vulnerabilities Across Ontario Neighborhoods:**
  - *Euclid Avenue Historic District & Downtown:* 1900s to 1930s Craftsman bungalows, Victorian cottages, and Spanish Colonial homes built with raised crawl space foundations, Douglas fir framing, and unreinforced concrete stem walls. Unventilated crawl spaces and low soil clearance allow subterranean termites direct access to mudsills and floor joists.
  - *Creekside & Central Ontario:* 1960s to 1980s single-family ranch homes built on slab-on-grade foundations. Settling concrete slabs create perimeter cracks and shrinkage around copper plumbing lines where termites enter interior partition framing.
  - *Ontario Ranch & Guasti:* Modern master-planned two-story homes with stucco exteriors. Buried stucco weep screeds and closely spaced landscape planters create hidden bridges for subterranean termites to bypass concrete foundations directly into wall framing.

### Pest Biology: Western Subterranean vs. Western Drywood Termites

Ontario homeowners face two distinct wood-destroying insects with different infestation behaviors:
- **Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** Soil-nesting insects that require ground moisture to survive. They build protective earthen mud shelter tubes over concrete foundation footings, stem walls, and plumbing conduits to reach floor joists, sills, and load-bearing studs.
- **Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*):** Wood-dwelling insects that nest inside dry structural timbers, roof framing, and window casings without needing soil contact or external moisture. They bore intricate internal galleries across structural timber and kick out distinctive grooved, sand-like fecal pellets (frass) through exit holes.

## California Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Civil Code § 1102 and SPCB Reports

California maintains some of the most rigorous real estate consumer protection statutes in the nation, governing mandatory disclosures and structural pest control inspections.

### The Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) & Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)

Under **California Civil Code § 1102 et seq.**, sellers of residential real property must provide prospective buyers with the statutory **Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)** and the California Association of Realtors (C.A.R.) **Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ)**:

- **TDS Section II.C (Environmental & Structural Hazards):** Explicitly asks whether the seller is aware of any:
  - *"Substances, materials, or products which may be an environmental hazard..."*
  - *"Room additions, structural modifications, or other alterations or repairs made without necessary permits..."*
  - *"Flooding, drainage, or grading problems..."*
- **SPQ Section V.A (Pests & Termites):** Specifically mandates the seller to disclose:
  - *"Any known current or past infestation or treatment for termites, wood-boring insects, rodents, or other pests."*
  - *"Any structural damage caused by termites, dry rot, or wood-destroying organisms."*
  - Requires the seller to attach copies of all historical termite inspection reports, completion notices, and repair contracts within their possession.

### California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) Branch 3 Reports

In California, official Wood Destroying Pest and Organisms (WDO) inspections are performed by Branch 3 licensed inspectors under the jurisdiction of the **California Structural Pest Control Board**:
- **Section 1 Items (Active Infestations & Damage):** Identifies active termite infestations, visible drywood frass, active subterranean mud tubes, and structural wood decay requiring immediate remediation or timber replacement.
- **Section 2 Items (Conducive Conditions):** Identifies conditions deemed likely to lead to infestation, such as earth-to-wood contact, faulty perimeter stucco grade, plumbing leaks, or standing water in subfloor crawl spaces.
- **Inaccessible Areas:** Section 4 of the SPCB report records obstructed areas, such as subfloor crawl spaces with less than 12-to-18 inches of clearance, areas behind fixed insulation, locked storage rooms, and enclosed attic vaults.

### Legal Precedent: *Easton v. Strassburger* and Seller Disclosure Duties

Under seminal California real estate rulings, including ***Easton v. Strassburger*, 152 Cal. App. 3d 90 (1984)** and ***Shapiro v. Sutherland*, 64 Cal. App. 4th 1534 (1998)**:
- Sellers and their listing agents have a non-waivable legal duty to disclose all known material facts affecting the value or desirability of the property.
- An "As-Is" provision in a C.A.R. Residential Purchase Agreement (RPA) does not relieve a seller from liability for intentional non-disclosure, active concealment, or fraudulent misrepresentation regarding structural termite damage.

### What Ontario Sellers Reveal vs. Strategically Conceal

Sellers in San Bernardino County often employ vague phrasing on the SPQ to downplay past pest history:
- *"Local spot treatment completed for drywood termites in 2022; cleared by exterminator."* (Conceals that localized treatment was chosen instead of recommended whole-house fumigation).
- *"Previous cosmetic repairs completed around patio overhang."* (Masks the structural replacement of drywood-eaten load-bearing beams).
- *"Clearance certificate obtained 3 years ago."* Fails to mention that Section 2 corrective recommendations (such as clearing buried weep screeds or fixing crawl space drainage) were never executed.

## The Manual Review Problem: Lost Warnings in 200-Page Closing Binders

During the standard 17-day inspection contingency period under the California Residential Purchase Agreement (C.A.R. Form RPA), buyers are inundated with technical documents:
- C.A.R. Form RPA Purchase Agreement and Advisory Addenda
- Statutory California Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and SPQ
- 45-to-80-page Comprehensive Property Inspection Report
- SPCB Branch 3 Structural Pest Control Inspection Report (WDO Report)
- Natural Hazard Disclosure (NHD) Report, Title Preliminary Report, and HOA Disclosures

### The Danger of Scattered Clues Across Separate Documents

Critical structural warnings rarely appear as an explicit, single warning. Instead, material clues are dispersed across separate disclosures:
- The Home Inspector notes on page 44: *"Minor subfloor slope and sagging observed along west dining room exterior wall."*
- The SPCB Termite Inspector writes under Inaccessible Areas: *"Low 10-inch crawl space clearance along west stem wall prevented physical inspection of mudsill."*
- The Seller checks "Yes" on SPQ Section V, noting: *"Local pest maintenance spray performed annually."*

Read independently, these notations seem manageable. But when cross-referenced, they reveal an alarming condition: subterranean termites entered through the inaccessible west crawl space, destroyed the load-bearing timber mudsill, and caused the subfloor slope, while the seller maintained only a cosmetic perimeter spray service.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Ontario

### 1. Are termites common in Ontario, California?
Yes. Both Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*) and Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*) are highly prevalent throughout Ontario, San Bernardino County, and the Inland Empire. Warm climate conditions, rich alluvial agricultural soils, and Santa Ana winds create high pest activity across both historic and modern homes.

### 2. What are California sellers legally required to disclose regarding termites?
Under California Civil Code § 1102 (TDS) and the C.A.R. Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ), sellers must disclose all known active infestations, prior treatments, historical structural damage, and provide copies of all past pest control reports and completion notices in their possession.

### 3. What is the difference between Section 1 and Section 2 on a California WDO report?
A California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) WDO report separates findings into Section 1 (active infestations, live termites, frass, and structural wood decay requiring immediate repair) and Section 2 (conducive conditions, such as earth-to-wood contact, plumbing leaks, or buried weep screeds that could foster future infestations).

### 4. Does homeowners insurance cover termite repairs in California?
No. Standard California homeowners insurance policies strictly exclude damage caused by termites, dry rot, and wood-destroying insects. Insurance carriers consider pest damage a preventable maintenance issue, requiring property owners to bear all structural and cosmetic repair costs.
