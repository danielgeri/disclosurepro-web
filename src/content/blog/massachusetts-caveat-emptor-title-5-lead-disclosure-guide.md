Assuming a Massachusetts home seller must hand you a comprehensive list of roof leaks, failed septic trenches, or active termites—before your **Offer to Purchase (OTP) inspection contingency** and **Purchase and Sale (P&S) agreement deadline** expire—regularly traps Bay State homebuyers with **$8,000 to $60,000+ in uninsured Title 5 septic rebuilds, mandatory Lead Law deleading, knob-and-tube rewires, and timber sill repairs**.

Unlike states with mandatory statewide disclosure questionnaires, Massachusetts remains a strict ***caveat emptor* ("buyer beware") jurisdiction** for private homeowners. However, four powerful statutory exceptions—**Title 5 septic compliance (310 CMR 15.000)**, the **Massachusetts Lead Law (M.G.L. c. 111, § 197)**, **Chapter 93A Consumer Protection duties** binding brokers and professional flippers, and the **2025 Massachusetts Home Inspection Waiver Ban (760 CMR 74.00)**—give buyers critical leverage when reviewing inspection PDFs, municipal records, and voluntary seller statements before signing the P&S.

## Why Massachusetts Has No Mandatory General Seller Disclosure Form (and Where Chapter 93A Steps In)

Under traditional Massachusetts common law, an individual homeowner selling their own residence has **no general statutory duty** to fill out a property condition checklist or volunteer every known flaw. Even when a seller signs the voluntary **Massachusetts Association of REALTORS® (MAR) Seller's Statement of Property Condition (SSPC)**, many listings leave sections blank or check *"Unknown."*

Yet Massachusetts is **not** a free-for-all for seller concealment. Three binding legal rules protect buyers:

### 1. Prohibition on Active Concealment & Direct Misrepresentation
A private Massachusetts seller cannot legally lie in response to a direct buyer question (for example, saying *"the basement has never leaked"* after pumping water out during spring snowmelt) and cannot **actively conceal** a defect—such as installing fresh drywall over rotted fieldstone sill beams or painting over active ceiling water stains before an open house.

### 2. Chapter 93A & 254 CMR 3.00: Strict Duties for Real Estate Brokers & Flippers
Under **M.G.L. c. 93A** (the Massachusetts Consumer Protection Act) and real estate licensing rules (**254 CMR 3.00**), **licensed real estate brokers, listing agents, developers, and professional house flippers** are held to a dramatically stricter standard than private homeowners:
- **Affirmative Duty to Disclose Known Material Defects:** Any defect that could influence a reasonable buyer's decision—such as an active subterranean termite infestation, cracked heat exchanger, buried heating oil tank, or unpermitted structural wall removal—**must** be disclosed by the listing broker if known.
- **Prior Collapsed Deals & Inspection Reports:** If an earlier buyer walked away from the property after an inspection uncovered a major structural, roof, or environmental defect—and that inspection report or defect notice was shared with the listing agent—the agent is legally obligated to disclose those known material facts to the next buyer.
- **Severe Statutory Penalties:** Violations of Chapter 93A expose brokers and commercial sellers to **double or triple (treble) damages plus reasonable attorney's fees**.

### 3. The Massachusetts Home Inspection Waiver Ban (760 CMR 74.00)
Under regulations enacted via the Massachusetts Affordable Homes Act (**760 CMR 74.00**), sellers and listing agents are **prohibited from conditioning the acceptance of an offer on a buyer waiving a home inspection**, and both parties must sign a mandatory **Home Inspection Rights Disclosure** no later than the first written contract (Offer to Purchase or P&S). Buyers can still negotiate how they handle findings, but sellers can no longer force blind pre-offer inspection waivers.

## Mandatory Massachusetts Disclosures Every Buyer Must Audit

Even though the MAR general condition statement is optional, Massachusetts law requires **four specific compliance documents and certifications** on residential sales:

### 1. Title 5 Septic System Inspection (310 CMR 15.000)
If the property relies on an on-site septic system or cesspool rather than municipal sewer, **310 CMR 15.000 (Title 5)** mandates an official inspection by a MassDEP-certified System Inspector at or before transfer:
- **Validity Windows:** A passing Title 5 report is valid for **2 years**—extended to **3 years** only if the seller provides documentation that the septic tank was pumped **annually** during that window.
- **Failed or "Conditionally Passing" Systems:** Cesspools within sensitive zones, high groundwater intrusion, or failed leaching fields trigger mandatory upgrade requirements (typically within **2 years** of failure, or faster if presenting an imminent public health hazard). Because Title 5 replacement systems in suburban and Cape Cod towns regularly cost **$25,000 to $60,000+**, never close on a failed Title 5 without a funded **1.5x escrow holdback** or major price credit.
- **Winter Weather Deferral:** If frozen ground or heavy snow prevents a pre-closing Title 5 inspection, Massachusetts permits deferring the inspection up to **6 months after closing** with written notice—creating massive risk for buyers unless an escrow holdback is written into the P&S.

### 2. The Massachusetts Lead Law (M.G.L. c. 111, § 197)
Massachusetts enforces one of the strictest lead paint laws in the United States for all residential properties built **before 1978**:
- **Mandatory Transfer Package:** Sellers and agents must deliver the official Massachusetts DPH **Property Transfer Lead Paint Notification** alongside any known lead inspection reports, Letters of Full Deleading Compliance, or Letters of Interim Control, and grant buyers the **10-day testing right**.
- **Strict Liability With Children Under Age 6:** If a child under 6 years old will reside in a pre-1978 Massachusetts home or rental unit, the owner is **legally required to delead** (or bring the property into strict compliance within 90 days of transfer under a valid Letter of Compliance)—and faces **strict liability** for lead poisoning regardless of actual knowledge. Always check the state's **Lead Safe Homes database** before finalizing your P&S.

### 3. Smoke & Carbon Monoxide Detector Certificate (M.G.L. c. 148, §§ 26F & 26F½)
Before closing, the seller must obtain a **Certificate of Compliance** from the local municipal Fire Department confirming that photoelectric/ionization smoke alarms and carbon monoxide detectors meet current Massachusetts spacing, battery, and hardwiring rules (including strict requirements for pre-1975 vs. post-1975 buildings).

### 4. Condominium 6(d) Certificate & Master Insurance / Reserve Review (M.G.L. c. 183A)
For Massachusetts condos—especially converted **triple-deckers** in Greater Boston, Somerville, Cambridge, and Worcester—the seller must deliver a **M.G.L. c. 183A § 6(d) Certificate** confirming zero unpaid condo dues or assessments. Equally important: demand the **Master Deed, Trust/Bylaws, past 2 years of trustee minutes, reserve account balance, and Master Insurance deductible** (older 3-unit condo associations routinely hold under $5,000 in reserves while facing an imminent $35,000 rubber roof or porch rebuild).

## Frequently Asked Questions About Massachusetts Real Estate Disclosures

### Is a seller property disclosure form required by law in Massachusetts?
No. Massachusetts is a *caveat emptor* ("buyer beware") state and does not mandate a general statewide seller property condition form for private residential homeowners. However, sellers must provide mandatory disclosures and certifications for **Title 5 septic systems**, **pre-1978 Lead Paint**, **smoke/CO detector compliance**, and **home inspection rights**—and licensed brokers and commercial flippers must disclose known material defects under **M.G.L. c. 93A**.

### What happens if a Massachusetts septic system fails a Title 5 inspection before closing?
If a septic system fails Title 5 under **310 CMR 15.000**, Massachusetts generally requires the system to be repaired or replaced within **2 years** (or sooner if posing an immediate public health threat). Most mortgage lenders will not fund closing on a failed Title 5 unless the seller repairs it prior to closing or funds a dedicated **1.5x repair escrow holdback** governed by a written Title 5 P&S rider.

### Does the Massachusetts Lead Law force me to delead a house I buy?
If you purchase a pre-1978 home in Massachusetts and **a child under age 6 will live in the property** (whether owner-occupied or rented), **M.G.L. c. 111, § 197** requires you to bring the home into full compliance (or obtain a Letter of Interim Control) within **90 days of taking title**. If no child under 6 lives there, deleading is not immediately required upon purchase, though you still inherit future compliance obligations if a young child moves in.

### Can a Massachusetts seller still demand that I waive my home inspection to win a bidding war?
No. Under Massachusetts regulations derived from the Affordable Homes Act (**760 CMR 74.00**), sellers and listing agents are legally prohibited from conditioning offer acceptance on a buyer waiving their right to a home inspection, and both parties must execute a mandatory written **Home Inspection Rights Disclosure** no later than the first written contract.
