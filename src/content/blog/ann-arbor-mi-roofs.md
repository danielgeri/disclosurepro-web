# The Ultimate Guide to Spotting Roof Damage in Ann Arbor Real Estate Disclosures

Renowned internationally as the intellectual, cultural, and healthcare epicenter of southeastern Michigan, Ann Arbor is celebrated for its extraordinary quality of life, picturesque riverfront topography along the Huron River, and the vibrant collegiate atmosphere generated by the University of Michigan (U-M). Affectionately known as "Tree Town" for its dense urban forestry of over 1.4 million trees, Ann Arbor boasts one of the most distinctive and historically preserved residential housing stocks in the Great Lakes region. Prospective homebuyers flock to Ann Arbor’s cherished neighborhoods: from the stately 1920s Craftsman, Dutch Colonial, and Tudor Revival residences of the Old West Side and Burns Park to the vibrant urban enclaves of Kerrytown and Water Hill, to the sprawling mid-century ranches of Arborview and the executive estates of Barton Hills.

Yet behind Ann Arbor’s lush tree canopies, progressive academic charm, and historic brick-paved streets lies an unforgiving Great Lakes winter climate that subjects residential roofing to relentless physical wear: **prolonged sub-freezing temperatures, heavy winter snowfalls punctuated by rapid freeze-thaw cycles that cultivate destructive ice dams along eaves, dense hardwood canopies that block solar drying and foster aggressive moss and lichen colonies, and complex century-old roof architectures plagued by failing copper valley flashings and unventilated attic spaces**.

Under Michigan real estate law—governed strictly by the **Michigan Seller Disclosure Act (SDA, MCL 565.951 et seq.)** and the statutory **Seller's Disclosure Statement (MCL 565.957)**—sellers are legally obligated to disclose all known material structural defects, prior water leaks, and roof repair histories. In Ann Arbor’s fiercely competitive housing market, however, sellers frequently conceal historic moisture intrusion behind fresh coats of primer, dismiss recurring winter ice dams as "temporary seasonal frost," or fail to disclose unpermitted shingle overlays that exceed structural load limits. Uncovering these costly vulnerabilities before your purchase contingency expires demands technical discernment and a comprehensive understanding of Michigan disclosure statutes.

## Why Ann Arbor Roofs Fail: Heavy Snow, Destructive Ice Dams, and Tree Canopies

Positioned in the lower peninsula of Michigan where polar air masses collide with moisture from the Great Lakes, Ann Arbor endures intense atmospheric and environmental conditions that aggressively degrade residential roofing systems.

### Severe Freeze-Thaw Cycles and Destructive Ice Damming
Ice damming represents the single most prevalent and costly structural roofing threat across Washtenaw County:
- **Attic Thermal Bypass and Snowmelt:** In older Ann Arbor homes—particularly historic 1920s homes in Old West Side and Burns Park with uninsulated attic crawlspaces—conditioned indoor heat escapes into the attic. This warms the underside of the upper roof decking, melting the bottom snow layer.
- **Eave Refreezing and Ice Dam Formation:** As meltwater runs down the roof pitch, it reaches the unheated roof eaves and overhanging gutters, where ambient temperatures remain well below 32°F. The water freezes instantly, creating an escalating ice ridge (ice dam).
- **Capillary Water Infiltration:** Meltwater pools behind the ice dam. Because asphalt shingles are designed solely to shed water downward by gravity, standing water wicks upward between shingle courses through capillary action. It bypasses standard felt underlayment, rotting structural roof decking, soaking rafter tails, and leaking into exterior wall assemblies.

### "Tree Town" Hardwood Canopies and Biological Decay
Ann Arbor's dense urban canopy of mature red oaks, sugar maples, and black walnuts creates a hostile microclimate for asphalt shingles:
- **Solar Shade and Moisture Retention:** Deep canopy shade prevents roof slopes—particularly north- and east-facing planes—from drying out after rain or snowmelt.
- **Moss and Lichen Root Penetration:** High humidity and persistent shade foster extensive carpets of green moss and lichens in shingle keyways. Lichen rhizoids anchor directly into the asphalt binder, breaking apart the shingle core and loosening surface mineral granules.
- **Organic Debris Dams:** Wet leaves, seed pods, and twigs accumulate in roof valleys, behind dormers, and around chimneys, forming moisture-retaining organic dams that rot underlying wood sheathing and corrode metal flashings.

### Complex Historic Roof Geometries and Flashing Degradation
Ann Arbor’s historic neighborhoods feature complex architectural rooflines:
- **Multiple Valleys, Hips, and Dormers:** Homes in Old West Side and Burns Park frequently feature gambrel roofs, eyebrow dormers, and intersecting gables. Each intersection relies on custom-formed copper or lead valley pans and step flashings.
- **Thermal Fatigue and Solder Seam Failure:** Over decades of extreme Great Lakes temperature swings (ranging from -15°F in winter to 95°F in summer), metal flashings expand and contract cyclically. Solder seams split, hairline stress fractures form in copper pans, and masonry mortar reglets loosen, allowing driving rain to silently infiltrate wall cavities.

### Heavy Snow Accumulation and Structural Dead-Load Stress
Winter storms sweeping across southern Michigan can deposit 12 to 20 inches of heavy, moisture-laden snow:
- **Excessive Structural Loading:** Wet snow can weigh up to 20 to 25 pounds per cubic foot. On multi-layer roofs where modern asphalt shingles were laid over old wood shakes or existing composition layers without removing the base layer, this immense dead load causes structural rafters to sag, crack, or twist out of plumb.
- **Snow Slide and Valley Scouring:** Sliding snow packs rip snow guards from roofs, tear gutters from fascia boards, and dislodge shingle tabs in high-velocity valleys.

## Understanding Michigan Real Estate Disclosure Laws

Michigan law provides robust consumer protections designed to ensure home purchasers are fully informed about the structural condition of a residential property.

### The Michigan Seller Disclosure Act (SDA, MCL 565.951 et seq.)
Under **MCL 565.954**, sellers of residential property consisting of one to four dwelling units must deliver a signed, written **Seller's Disclosure Statement (MCL 565.957)** to the prospective purchaser before an offer to purchase is accepted.

The statutory disclosure statement explicitly mandates disclosures concerning the roof:
- **Roof Covering and Age:** The seller must state the type of roof covering (e.g., composition shingle, slate, standing seam metal) and its approximate age.
- **Known Leaks and Defects:** The seller must affirmatively declare whether they are aware of any current or past roof leaks, water intrusion, or structural defects in the roof, ceilings, or walls.
- **Repair History:** The seller must disclose details regarding all roof repairs, patching, or replacements performed during their period of ownership.
- **Unpermitted Work:** The seller must disclose any structural additions, dormer construction, or reroofing performed without required municipal building permits.

### Common Law Fraud and Landmark Case Law (*Bergen v. Baker*)
Michigan courts strictly enforce truthfulness on statutory disclosure forms:
- In the landmark ruling ***Bergen v. Baker*, 264 Mich. App. 376, 691 N.W.2d 770 (2004)**, the Michigan Court of Appeals held that a seller who makes false statements or conceals known defects on the Seller's Disclosure Statement can be held liable for common law fraud, silent fraud (fraudulent concealment), and innocent misrepresentation.
- The court established that an "as-is" clause in a Michigan purchase contract does not shield a seller who intentionally conceals known roof leaks or makes affirmative misrepresentations regarding property condition.
- In ***M&D, Inc. v. McConkey*, 231 Mich. App. 22, 585 N.W.2d 33 (1998)**, Michigan courts affirmed that taking active steps to hide defects—such as painting over water-stained plaster ceilings without repairing the underlying roof leak—constitutes actionable silent fraud.
- Defrauded homebuyers can seek compensatory damages (full cost of necessary roof replacement and interior restoration) and litigation expenses.

### City of Ann Arbor Building Codes and Permitting Regulations
The **City of Ann Arbor Building Services Department** enforces the 2015/2018 Michigan Residential Code (MRC) with local municipal amendments:
- **Building Permits Required:** A residential building permit is legally mandatory for all reroofing projects exceeding 100 square feet (one roofing square) or involving full tear-off and replacement.
- **Mandatory Ice Barrier Underlayment:** Under MRC Section R905.1.2 for Climate Zone 5A (which includes Washtenaw County), an ice-barrier membrane (self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen) is mandatory. It must extend from the lowest roof edge upward to a point at least 24 inches inside the interior heated wall line.
- **Decking Inspections:** When existing shingles are torn off, all rotted, delaminated, or damaged wood sheathing must be replaced before installing new underlayment.
- **Maximum Number of Layers:** The Michigan Residential Code permits a maximum of two layers of roof coverings. If two layers already exist, complete tear-off down to the wood deck is required.

## The Manual Review Problem: Navigating Michigan Due Diligence

Under standard Michigan Association of Realtors purchase agreements, buyers typically negotiate an **Inspection Contingency Period of 7 to 10 calendar days**.

During this compressed evaluation window, prospective buyers must digest a substantial volume of technical documentation:
- The completed Michigan MCL 565.957 Seller's Disclosure Statement.
- C.L.U.E. insurance loss history reports showing previous winter storm and water intrusion claims.
- Historical building permit records from the City of Ann Arbor Building Services Department.
- General home inspection reports spanning 45 to 70 pages that routinely disclaim comprehensive roof evaluations due to roof pitch, snow cover, or inaccessible attic spaces.

### Vague Phrasing Sellers Use to Disguise Roof Damage
Sellers in Ann Arbor frequently use subtle, ambiguous language on disclosure forms to downplay serious roofing defects and avoid paying for costly replacements:
- *"Roof routinely maintained by reputable local contractor; in good seasonal condition."* (Conceals chronic valley leaks, split copper solder seams, and rotted skip sheathing).
- *"Cosmetic moss removed from shaded north slope."* (Disguises moisture damage, loosened granules, and rotting wood decking beneath).
- *"Attic insulation recently upgraded for energy efficiency."* (Masks historical moisture staining and hidden black mold in rafter bays).
- *"Minor ice damming occurs during historic 100-year snowstorms; handled with roof rake."* (Hides the total absence of a code-mandated ice-barrier membrane).

### The Purchase
David and Sarah purchased a 1922 two-story Craftsman home in Ann Arbor's Old West Side for $520,000. The property featured original chestnut woodwork, a wide covered front porch, mature sugar maple shade trees, and an architectural shingle roof that looked neat and attractive from the street.

On the Michigan Seller's Disclosure Statement (MCL 565.957), the seller marked "No" to active roof leaks and answered "Yes" to repairs, adding a handwritten note: *"Roof evaluated in 2023; local contractor replaced several valley shingles and resealed dormer flashings. No active leaks."*

During their 8-day inspection period, David and Sarah hired a licensed Michigan home inspector. The inspection took place on a dry, sunny afternoon in October. The inspector viewed the steep-pitch roof from an extension ladder at the gutter line and noted in the report: *"Architectural shingles appear in satisfactory condition with normal granule wear consistent with age. Attic crawlspace partially obscured by blown-in cellulose insulation."* David and Sarah finalized the transaction.

### The January Blizzard and Second-Floor Study Collapse
Fourteen months later, in January, a major Great Lakes winter storm dumped 18 inches of heavy, wet snow on Washtenaw County, followed immediately by sustained sub-zero temperatures and a rapid February thaw:
- **Massive Ice Dam Inundation:** Poorly insulated attic knee walls in the finished second-floor study allowed conditioned heat to escape into the attic. A massive 9-inch-thick ice dam built up along the eaves and internal roof valley.
- **Water Penetration Behind Shingles:** Meltwater ponded behind the ice dam and backed up 7 feet up the roof slope. The roof lacked a code-mandated self-adhering ice-barrier membrane; shingles had been installed over cheap 15-pound saturated felt paper that had rotted years prior.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Roof Issues in Ann Arbor, MI

### 1. Does the City of Ann Arbor require a building permit for roof replacement?
Yes. The City of Ann Arbor Building Services Department requires a residential building permit for all reroofing projects exceeding 100 square feet (one square) or involving a complete tear-off down to the decking. Installations must comply with the 2015/2018 Michigan Residential Code (MRC). Building inspectors evaluate structural decking condition and verify that code-mandated ice-barrier underlayment is properly installed prior to project sign-off.

### 2. Can a seller in Michigan be held legally liable for hiding roof leaks?
Yes. Under the Michigan Seller Disclosure Act (MCL 565.951 et seq.) and the Michigan Court of Appeals ruling in *Bergen v. Baker*, sellers are legally liable for fraudulent misrepresentation and silent fraud if they fail to disclose known roof defects on the statutory disclosure form. If a seller conceals water leaks behind cosmetic paint or fails to disclose prior ice dam intrusion, an "as-is" clause will not protect them, and buyers can sue for full repair damages and litigation expenses.

### 3. What is an ice barrier membrane, and why is it mandatory in Ann Arbor?
An ice barrier membrane is a self-adhering polymer-modified bitumen underlayment that adheres directly to the roof sheathing. Under Michigan Residential Code Section R905.1.2 for Climate Zone 5A, it must be installed along all roof eaves, extending from the lowest edge upward to a point at least 24 inches inside the interior heated wall line. This membrane seals tightly around roofing nails and prevents meltwater from entering the home when ice dams form during freezing winter storms.

### 4. How does Ann Arbor’s dense tree canopy impact asphalt shingle lifespan?
Ann Arbor’s mature oak and maple tree canopies create continuous shade and high humidity, preventing roof slopes from drying completely. This fosters moss and lichen growth that anchors into the asphalt binder, loosens protective mineral granules, and leads to premature curling and shingle decay. Regular professional moss removal and trimming tree canopies to maintain a minimum 10-foot clearance from the roofline are essential to preserve shingle integrity.
