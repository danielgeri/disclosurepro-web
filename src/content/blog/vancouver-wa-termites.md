## The Hidden Threat: Termites in Vancouver and Clark County

Vancouver sits in the Columbia River Basin across from Portland, Oregon. Our local ecosystem features heavy annual precipitation (averaging over 42 inches of rain per year), extended damp and overcast autumn and winter months, and dense clay loam soils that retain high ground moisture. These environmental factors create prime conditions for multiple wood-destroying organisms.

### Why Pacific Northwest Housing Stock and Moisture Elevate Pest Risk

Several unique regional construction practices, lumber materials, and environmental factors contribute to intense termite activity across Vancouver:

- **Persistent Moisture and Unencapsulated Crawl Spaces:** Heavy seasonal rainfall frequently causes moisture to seep into unencapsulated dirt crawl spaces throughout Clark County. Inadequate foundation ventilation traps humid air beneath subfloors, driving wood moisture content above 20%—a critical threshold that actively attracts subterranean and dampwood termite colonies.
- **Daylight Basements and Hillside Surface Runoff:** In hillside neighborhoods like Felida and Salmon Creek, homes are frequently built with daylight basements or stepped foundation stem walls. Rainwater runoff flowing down slopes pools against downhill foundation walls, creating damp conditions where subterranean termites construct mud shelter tubes directly into structural mudsills.
- **Douglas Fir Framing and Earth-to-Wood Contact:** Traditional Pacific Northwest construction relies heavily on native Douglas fir framing. While Douglas fir provides exceptional structural load-bearing capacity, it has low natural resistance to wood-boring insects once exposed to prolonged moisture. Wood siding, deck posts, or landscape timbers contacting soil provide immediate entry corridors.
- **Architectural Vulnerabilities Across Vancouver Neighborhoods:**
  - *Arnada & Hough (Historic Downtown):* 1890s to 1930s Craftsman bungalows and Queen Anne homes built over unreinforced masonry or brick pier foundations. Decaying mortar and low crawl space clearances allow subterranean termites to build mud tubes into floor joists undetected.
  - *Felida & Salmon Creek:* 1970s to 1990s daylight ranch homes and split-levels. Stepped concrete stem walls and expansion joints between garage slabs and main framing provide subterranean entry pathways.
  - *Fisher’s Landing & Cascade Park:* 1990s to 2000s two-story suburban homes. Exterior siding terminating too close to finished soil grade allows subterranean termites to bypass concrete stem walls.

### Pest Biology: Subterranean vs. Pacific Dampwood Termites

Vancouver properties face simultaneous pressure from two distinct wood-destroying species:
- **Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** Colony-based underground dwellers that construct tubular earthen mud shelter tunnels across foundation concrete stem walls to reach structural wood framing, feeding on soft springwood along the grain.
- **Pacific Dampwood Termites (*Zootermopsis angusticollis*):** Among the largest termite species in the world, dampwood termites infest damp, decaying wood in crawl spaces, rim joists, and exterior decking. Unlike subterranean species, they do not construct mud shelter tubes, nesting directly inside moisture-saturated timbers and excavating massive internal galleries.

## Washington Real Estate Disclosure Laws: RCW 64.06 and Seller Obligations

Washington law provides comprehensive statutory disclosure requirements to ensure residential buyers receive clear information regarding structural and environmental defects.

### Washington Revised Code (RCW) 64.06: The Seller Disclosure Statement (Form 17)

Under **Revised Code of Washington (RCW) 64.06**, sellers of residential real property must provide prospective buyers with the completed **Seller Disclosure Statement (Form 17)** within five business days of mutual contract acceptance:

- **Section 7: Environmental & Pest Conditions (Question 7.C):** The seller must disclose:
  - *"Are there any pest or insect infestations on the property?"*
  - *"Are there any active or past wood-destroying organism infestations (termites, carpenter ants, wood-boring beetles)?"*
  - *"Has the property experienced structural damage caused by wood-destroying organisms?"*
  - *"Has the property received any pest control chemical treatments or structural damage repairs?"*
- **Section 4: Structural Conditions:** Mandates disclosure of standing water in crawl spaces, foundation settling, masonry cracking, or subfloor movement—conditions that directly accelerate pest colonization.
- **Buyer Rescission Rights:** If a seller fails to provide Form 17 within statutory timelines, the buyer has the statutory right to rescind the purchase agreement and receive a full return of their earnest money deposit.

### Washington Legal Standards: *Alejandre v. Bull* and Fraud Exceptions

In Washington, real estate non-disclosure disputes are interpreted under the framework established by the Washington Supreme Court in ***Alejandre v. Bull*, 159 Wn.2d 674 (2007)**:
- While contract provisions and independent inspection clauses place due diligence burdens on buyers, a seller remains liable under common law for affirmative fraudulent misrepresentation or intentional concealment of known latent material defects.
- An "As-Is" provision in a Washington purchase agreement does not shield a seller who actively conceals known structural pest damage or makes false representations on Form 17.

### The Washington Structural Pest Inspection (SPI) Report

In Washington, official wood-destroying organism inspections must be performed by a licensed Structural Pest Inspector (SPI) certified by the **Washington State Department of Agriculture (WSDA)** under **RCW 15.58**:
- **Findings Section:** Details active infestations, previous infestation evidence, visible structural wood damage, or past treatment drill holes in concrete.
- **Conducive Conditions Section:** Highlights conditions that foster pest infestations, including standing crawl space water, earth-to-wood contact, failing vapor barriers, and clogged foundation vents.
- **Inaccessible Areas:** Explicitly records zones that were obstructed from visual inspection, such as low-clearance crawl spaces under 18 inches, crawl spaces blocked by fiberglass insulation, or fully insulated attics.

### What Vancouver Sellers Reveal vs. Conceal

Sellers in Clark County frequently attempt to downplay past pest issues on Form 17:
- *"Local preventative treatment performed in crawl space in 2022."* (Conceals that subterranean termite mud tubes were found on interior stem walls).
- *"Floor framing reinforced during bathroom remodel."* (Hides the fact that structural rim joists had been hollowed out by dampwood termites).
- *"Sold As-Is."* Under Washington law, selling "As-Is" does not excuse a seller from their statutory duty to truthfully complete Form 17 or protect them from liability for fraudulent concealment.

- Clark County Assessor records, Preliminary Title Commitments, and Radon Reports

### The Risk of Disconnected Clues

Material termite hazards rarely appear as an explicit, single warning in real estate closing packets. Instead, critical indicators are fragmented across independent reports:
- The Home Inspector writes on page 38: *"Crawl space vapor barrier is displaced; rim joists along north stem wall show elevated moisture readings."*
- The WSDA Pest Inspector notes in the SPI report: *"Fiberglass batt insulation beneath main floor joists prevented direct visual examination of rim joists."*
- The Seller checks "No" to structural damage on Form 17, writing: *"Annual pest control maintenance contract maintained with local provider."*

When evaluated separately, each statement seems routine. But when cross-referenced, they reveal a dangerous scenario: heavy winter runoff created standing moisture along the stem wall, subterranean and dampwood termites entered behind the sagging fiberglass insulation, and hollowed out the rim joists and subflooring while the homeowner maintained only a routine exterior spray service.

Craig and Heather purchased a 1978 daylight ranch home on a sloping lot in Felida for $540,000. On the Washington Form 17 disclosure, the seller checked "Yes" to prior pest treatments, adding a note: *"Preventative crawl space treatment performed in 2022; transferable service warranty."* The seller marked "No" to any known structural damage.

During their inspection period, Craig and Heather hired a general home inspector and ordered an official WSDA SPI pest inspection. The pest inspector found no live termites in the open crawl space walkway, but noted in the SPI report that low 16-inch clearance and thick fiberglass batt insulation prevented direct visual inspection of the downhill rim joists and sill plates. Reassured by the transferable warranty, Craig and Heather completed the purchase.

Seven months later, Heather noticed that the baseboards in the downstairs daylight family room were soft and spongy, with a slight slope developing in the subfloor above. When a licensed structural framing contractor removed the crawl space insulation along the downhill foundation wall, they uncovered extensive subterranean and dampwood termite devastation.

Subterranean termites had climbed through an unsealed cold joint between the foundation wall and the daylight slab, while dampwood termites colonized moisture-saturated Douglas fir rim joists above a saturated soil pocket. Over a four-year period, they had destroyed 20 linear feet of the main structural rim joist, hollowed out nine 2x10 floor joists, and eaten through the subflooring beneath the main-floor kitchen. The seller’s 2022 "crawl space treatment" had been an inexpensive surface spray chosen after a licensed inspector recommended timber sistering that the seller declined to complete before listing the property.

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Vancouver

### 1. Are termites common in Vancouver, Washington?
Yes. Western Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes hesperus*) and Pacific Dampwood Termites (*Zootermopsis angusticollis*) are prevalent throughout Vancouver and Clark County. High annual rainfall, unencapsulated dirt crawl spaces, daylight basement foundations, and native Douglas fir framing create prime conditions for termite colonies to thrive.

### 2. What are Washington sellers legally required to disclose regarding termites?
Under RCW 64.06 (Form 17), sellers must disclose all known active or past pest infestations, structural timber damage, historical chemical treatments, and crawl space moisture problems. Failing to disclose known material defects exposes sellers to liability for fraudulent concealment.

### 3. What is a WSDA Structural Pest Inspection (SPI) report?
A Structural Pest Inspection (SPI) report is an official inspection document prepared by a pest inspector licensed by the Washington State Department of Agriculture (WSDA). It details visible evidence of active pests, prior damage, past treatment marks, conducive moisture conditions, and inaccessible structural zones.

### 4. Does homeowners insurance cover termite damage in Washington?
No. Standard Washington homeowners insurance policies exclude damage caused by termites, dry rot, and wood-destroying insects. Insurance carriers classify termite infestations as preventable property maintenance issues, requiring homeowners to pay for all structural, framing, and cosmetic repairs out of pocket.
