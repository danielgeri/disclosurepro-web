## The Hidden Threat: Desert Termites in Peoria and the Northwest Valley

Peoria spans from the urbanized Salt River Valley northward into the Sonoran Desert foothills surrounding Lake Pleasant. Our environment features extreme summer temperatures exceeding 115°F, alkaline caliche soils, heavy summer monsoon cloudbursts, and expansive post-tension slab foundations—factors that create unique behavior patterns among local wood-destroying insects.

### Why Peoria Housing Stock and Desert Geology Elevate Pest Risk

Several architectural, geological, and landscaping factors make Peoria residential properties particularly susceptible to structural termite activity:

- **Post-Tension Slabs and Plumbing Penetrations:** Modern Peoria homes are predominantly built on monolithic or post-tension concrete slabs. When concrete cures and settles, minute contraction gaps form around copper water lines, ABS drainpipes, and bathtub block-outs. Subterranean termites require a gap of only 1/32 of an inch to bypass concrete slabs and enter interior wall cavities.
- **Buried Stucco Weep Screeds:** Arizona building code requires metal weep screeds along the bottom edge of exterior stucco walls to allow trapped moisture to escape. In neighborhoods like Vistancia and Fletcher Heights, homeowners frequently install desert landscaping, decorative river rock, artificial turf, or concrete paver walkways directly above the weep screed. This allows termites to enter exterior wall framing completely concealed behind the stucco shell.
- **Irrigation and Microclimates in Desert Landscaping:** Drip irrigation lines running to desert shrubs, palm trees, and citrus plants along the exterior foundation perimeter create moist soil pockets. In an arid desert climate, these irrigated foundation zones attract foraging subterranean colonies from hundreds of feet away.
- **Architectural Vulnerabilities Across Peoria Neighborhoods:**
  - *Old Town Peoria:* Built from the 1940s through the 1970s, older homes often feature raised wooden floor joists, unreinforced masonry stems, or conventional concrete slabs without modern chemical pre-treatments.
  - *Westbrook Village:* 1980s and 1990s single-story golf course patio homes featuring expansive wood-framed covered patios, decorative wooden trellis structures, and enclosed courtyards with earth-to-wood contact points.
  - *Fletcher Heights & Sonoran Mountain Ranch:* Late 1990s and 2000s two-story stucco homes with engineered floor trusses and interior load-bearing timber partitions that can be severely degraded before exterior mud tubes become visible.

### Pest Biology: Desert Subterranean (*Heterotermes aureus*) and Drywood Termites

Peoria homeowners face threats from multiple wood-destroying species:
- **Desert Subterranean Termites (*Heterotermes aureus*):** The most widespread pest in Maricopa County. Highly adapted to desert extremes, they tolerate higher temperatures and drier conditions than eastern subterranean species, constructing slender, parchment-like mud tubes across stem walls and interior drywall.
- **Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*):** Unlike subterranean species, drywood termites do not require soil contact. They enter homes during late-spring and summer swarms, infesting exposed roof eaves, fascia boards, and attic trusses, expelling distinctive hexagonal fecal pellets (frass).
- **Arid-Land Subterranean Termites (*Reticulitermes tibialis*):** Often found in riverbeds, wash corridors, and foothill developments, active during wet spring months following winter rains.

## Arizona Real Estate Disclosure Laws: The SPDS and Hill v. Jones Mandate

Arizona real estate transactions are governed by strict disclosure requirements established by the Arizona Department of Real Estate (ADRE) and state judicial precedent.

### The Arizona Residential Seller's Property Disclosure Statement (SPDS)

The standard **Residential SPDS** created by the Arizona Association of Realtors (AAR) is the cornerstone of seller disclosures in Maricopa County:

- **Section 4 (Environmental/Pests):** Specifically obligates the seller to answer:
  - *"Are you aware of any past or present insect, termite, or other wood-destroying organism infestations on the property?"*
  - *"Are you aware of any past or present damage to the property caused by insects, termites, or other wood-destroying organisms?"*
  - *"Are you aware of any past or present chemical treatments for termites or other wood-destroying organisms?"*
  - *"Is the property currently under a termite warranty or treatment contract?"*
- **Section 2 (Building & Safety Information):** Mandates disclosure of roof leaks, exterior stucco cracks, and plumbing leaks, which create the moisture conditions that sustain subterranean termite colonies.

### The Landmark Arizona Precedent: *Hill v. Jones* (1986)

In Arizona real estate law, the landmark case regarding termite disclosures is ***Hill v. Jones*, 151 Ariz. 81, 725 P.2d 1115 (Ariz. Ct. App. 1986)**:
- In *Hill v. Jones*, the Arizona Court of Appeals established that where a seller knows of facts materially affecting the value or desirability of the property—specifically prior termite infestations and structural repairs—which are not known to or readily observable by the buyer, the seller has an affirmative legal duty to disclose them.
- The court ruled that an "As-Is" clause or standard integration clause in an Arizona purchase contract does not shield a seller from fraudulent non-disclosure or intentional concealment of past termite activity.

### The Official Arizona WDIIR (Wood-Destroying Insect Inspection Report)

Official termite inspections in Arizona are regulated by the **Arizona Department of Agriculture Office of Pest Management (OPM)** and must be executed on standardized **Form WDIIR 1364/1365**:
- **Section 8 (Findings):** Notes active infestation, previous infestation evidence, visible structural damage, and past drill marks along perimeter stem walls or garage slabs.
- **Section 9 (Inaccessible Areas):** Highlights obstructed zones, including areas where stucco weep screeds are buried by soil or pavers, vaulted ceilings lacking attic crawl access, and areas behind attached cabinetry.

### What Peoria Sellers Reveal vs. Conceal

Peoria sellers often attempt to minimize past termite history on the SPDS:
- *"Perimeter treated in 2021 for routine pest maintenance."* (Conceals that mud tubes were discovered inside master bathroom wall cavities).
- *"Spot treatment applied along garage stem wall."* (Downplays localized chemical injection that failed to address full subterranean activity beneath the main slab).
- *"No active termites known to seller."* Sellers may claim lack of current knowledge while concealing historical treatment reports, subterranean drilling history, or past structural damage.

## The Manual Review Problem: Lost Clues in 200-Page Closing Packets

During the standard 10-day inspection period under the Arizona Association of Realtors Residential Resale Real Estate Purchase Contract, buyers must absorb an overwhelming amount of paperwork:
- AAR Residential Purchase Contract and Local Addenda
- Seller's Property Disclosure Statement (SPDS)
- 40-to-75-page General Home Inspection Report with infrared thermal scans
- Arizona OPM WDIIR Wood-Destroying Insect Inspection Report
- Maricopa County Assessor maps, HOA CC&R documents, and Title Commitments

### How Critical Warnings Get Missed

Warning signs of severe termite activity are frequently split across separate reports:
- The Home Inspector notes on page 41: *"Small drywall patch and soft baseboard observed in master closet near plumbing wall."*
- The WDIIR Pest Inspector writes in Section 9: *"Decorative brick pavers along east foundation wall obstruct visual access to stucco weep screed."*
- The Seller checks "Yes" to prior treatment on the SPDS, adding: *"Routine warranty renewed annually."*

Separately, these notes appear insignificant. But when cross-referenced, they outline a dangerous defect: subterranean termites traveled up behind the buried weep screed, entered the master closet plumbing wall, and hollowed out the structural 2x4 framing behind the freshly painted drywall patch.

Eight months later, Carlos noticed that the drywall around the master bathroom doorframe was rippling, and pressing against the baseboard caused the wood trim to crumble into paper-thin flakes. ## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Peoria

### 1. How common are termites in Peoria, Arizona?
Desert Subterranean Termites (*Heterotermes aureus*) and Western Drywood Termites (*Incisitermes minor*) are extremely common throughout Peoria and Maricopa County. Subterranean colonies nest deep in desert soil and exploit foundation joints, plumbing sleeves, and buried stucco weep screeds to infest structural framing.

### 2. What does Arizona law require sellers to disclose about termites?
Under Arizona case law (*Hill v. Jones*) and Arizona Department of Real Estate guidelines, sellers must disclose all known past and present termite infestations, structural damage, chemical treatments, and active warranty bonds on Section 4 of the Residential SPDS.

### 3. What is a WDIIR in Arizona?
A WDIIR (Wood-Destroying Insect Inspection Report) is the standardized state form (Form 1364/1365) issued by inspectors licensed by the Arizona Department of Agriculture Office of Pest Management (OPM). It outlines visible active pests, prior treatment marks, structural damage, and inaccessible building areas.

### 4. Does homeowners insurance cover termite repairs in Arizona?
No. Homeowners insurance policies in Arizona universally exclude termite damage and wood-destroying insect repairs. Insurance companies deem termite prevention a standard homeowner maintenance duty, leaving all structural and cosmetic restoration costs to the property owner.
