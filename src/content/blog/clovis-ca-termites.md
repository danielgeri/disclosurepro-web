## The Hidden Threat: Subterranean and Drywood Termites Across Clovis, CA

Clovis' semi-arid climate, valley soil mechanics, and diverse residential architectural eras combine to create specific vulnerabilities for residential framing.

### How Central Valley Heat Waves, Alluvial Soil, and Framing Types Foster Infestations

Several localized environmental and construction factors make Clovis homes prime targets for wood-destroying organisms:

- **Thermal Extremes and Moisture Gradients:** With summer air temperatures routinely surpassing 105°F and relative humidity dropping into single digits, native subterranean termites cannot survive near the baked surface soil. They burrow deep into the subsoil beneath foundation footings. When residential drip irrigation lines, lawn sprinklers, or leaking hose bibbs saturate the soil adjacent to concrete slabs, subterranean termites construct earthen shelter tubes directly up stem walls to reach cool, moist wood framing.
- **Winter Tule Fog and Moisture Accumulation:** From November through February, the San Joaquin Valley experiences dense Tule fog—stagnant, moisture-laden air masses that saturate roof eaves, exposed rafter tails, and unpainted fascias. This seasonal moisture softens exterior wood and creates optimal conditions for fungal wood decay (dry rot), which serves as a prime attractant and entry point for opportunistic drywood termites.
- **Architectural Stock and Construction Vulnerabilities:**
  - *Old Town Clovis & Tarpey Village:* 1920s to 1950s Craftsman cottages and California ranch homes constructed over shallow raised crawlspaces. Low soil clearance, inadequate cross-ventilation, and uninsulated crawlspaces allow subterranean termites direct access to redwood mudsills, floor joists, and subfloor sheathing.
  - *Dry Creek & Buchanan Areas:* 1980s and 1990s single-family homes featuring vaulted ceilings, exterior stucco siding, and complex concrete tile roof systems. Concrete roof tiles often crack or shift under thermal expansion, allowing windblown drywood swarmers to penetrate underlayment and establish colonies directly in attic trusses.
  - *Harlan Ranch & Loma Vista:* Contemporary 2000s to 2020s two-story stucco homes built on monolithic post-tensioned concrete slabs. Subterranean termites exploit plumbing block-outs (especially beneath primary bathroom garden tubs and laundry rough-ins) to bypass perimeter termiticide treatments and attack interior load-bearing studs.

### Pest Biology: Western Subterranean vs. Western Drywood Termites

Understanding the biological profiles of Central Valley timber pests is crucial when interpreting disclosure disclosures:

- **Western Subterranean Termite (*Reticulitermes hesperus*):** The primary wood-destroying pest in Central California. A mature colony can house hundreds of thousands of workers living in subterranean nests. They build vertical mud shelter tubes to cross non-wood obstacles, devouring softwood along the structural grain and leaving thin outer shells of timber intact.
- **Arid-land Subterranean Termite (*Reticulitermes tibialis*):** Highly prevalent in the arid agricultural lands of Fresno County. Adapted to low-moisture environments, they tunnel deeply beneath foundation slabs and can exploit minor hairline stress cracks in concrete footings to access structural sill plates.
- **Western Drywood Termite (*Incisitermes minor*):** Entirely independent of ground soil moisture, drywood termites nest inside structural rafters, window jambs, and fascia boards. They extract moisture directly from ambient air and ingested wood. Colonies produce tiny, grooved, football-shaped fecal pellets that accumulate in distinctive conical piles beneath active infestation sites.
- **Swarming Periods in Clovis:** Subterranean termites swarm on warm, sunny afternoons following early spring rain events from February through April. Drywood termites swarm in explosive flights on scorching, sunny afternoons in September and October, frequently penetrating attic gable vents and eave soffits.

## California Real Estate Disclosure Laws: Civil Code § 1102 and SPCB Standards

California maintains some of the nation’s most stringent consumer disclosure statutes, placing affirmative legal obligations on residential sellers to disclose known property defects.

### California Civil Code § 1102 and the Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS)

Under **California Civil Code § 1102 et seq.**, sellers of residential properties containing one to four units must provide a completed Real Estate Transfer Disclosure Statement (TDS) and Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ):

- **Section II.C of the TDS Form:** Explicitly mandates that the seller state whether they are aware of any *"Substances, materials, or products which may be an environmental hazard... or Wood Destroying Pests/Organisms."*
- **The Seller Property Questionnaire (SPQ):** Section V.A of the standard California Association of Realtors (C.A.R.) SPQ form directly inquires: *"Are you (Seller) aware of: (1) Any past or present infestation, damage, or treatment of termites, dry rot, or other wood-destroying organisms? (2) Any past or present structural repairs or alterations made to address pest damage?"*
- **Duty of Good Faith and Truthfulness:** Disclosures must be made in good faith. A seller who answers "No" despite having previously observed termite droppings, received pest treatment bids, or completed cosmetic cover-ups is liable for intentional misrepresentation or fraudulent concealment.
- **Rescission Rights:** Under California Civil Code § 1102.3, if the TDS or any material amendment is delivered after an offer is executed, the buyer has a statutory right to terminate the contract within three days after personal delivery (or five days after delivery by mail/electronic transmission).

### The California Structural Pest Control Board (SPCB) WDO Inspection Report

In California, pest inspections are conducted by licensed Branch 3 Structural Pest Control operators and documented on a standardized Structural Pest Control Inspection Report:

- **Section 1 Items:** Conditions with active infestations or infections (active subterranean termites, drywood termites, wood-decay fungus) or structural damage resulting from them. In standard purchase negotiations, buyers frequently request sellers to pay for complete Section 1 clearance.
- **Section 2 Items:** Conditions deemed conducive to infestation or infection, such as earth-to-wood contact, faulty plumbing leaks, unvented crawlspaces, or broken stucco weep screeds, where no active damage is yet evident.
- **Notice of Work Completed (Form 43M-44):** If the seller performed pest remediation prior to listing, California law requires them to furnish copies of all previous inspection reports, structural certifications, and official Notices of Work Completed filed with the SPCB within the preceding two years.

### Deceptive Disclosure Phrasing in Central Valley Transactions

Savvy buyers in Clovis should be alert to ambiguous seller statements:

- *"Home inspected by pest company two years ago; minor preventative touch-ups done."* (Often obscures uncompleted Section 1 structural repairs that were deemed too costly).
- *"Fascia boards repaired and repainted prior to listing."* (Frequently masks drywood termite hollowing along eaves without addressing internal attic framing).
- *"Previous localized spot treatment applied in garage wall."* (Fails to disclose whether subterranean termites had migrated into adjacent living room floor framing).

## Frequently Asked Questions about Termites in Clovis, CA

### 1. What is the difference between Section 1 and Section 2 on a California pest report?
In California, a Branch 3 Wood Destroying Pest and Organisms Inspection Report classifies findings into two categories. Section 1 covers conditions with active infestations or infections (live termites, beetles, or active wood decay fungus) and resulting damage that requires immediate treatment or repair. Section 2 covers conditions conducive to infestation (such as plumbing leaks, earth-to-wood contact, or faulty subfloor ventilation) that should be corrected to prevent future pest invasion.

### 2. Can a seller in Clovis legally sell a home "AS IS" without fixing termite damage?
Yes, a home can be sold "AS IS" in California. However, an "AS IS" clause only means the seller is not contractually obligated to pay for repairs; it does not exempt the seller from California Civil Code § 1102 disclosure requirements. The seller is still legally required to disclose all known termite infestations, previous treatments, and structural damage on the TDS and SPQ forms.

### 3. Why are both subterranean and drywood termites a problem in Clovis?
Clovis experiences extreme weather dynamics that accommodate both major termite families. Scorching summer droughts and expansive clay soils drive subterranean termites deep underground to seek moisture around irrigated foundations and plumbing pipes. Meanwhile, sustained summer heat waves create ideal swarming conditions for drywood termites, which enter attic vents and roof fascias directly from the air without needing soil contact.

### 4. Is whole-house tenting always required for drywood termites in Clovis?
Not always, but whole-house fumigation (tenting with sulfuryl fluoride) remains the gold standard for eradicating widespread or inaccessible drywood termite colonies throughout roof trusses and wall framing. Localized spot treatments (such as chemical injection or heat treatments) can be effective for isolated, accessible infestations, but they carry a high risk of missing hidden colonies nesting deeper within wall cavities.
